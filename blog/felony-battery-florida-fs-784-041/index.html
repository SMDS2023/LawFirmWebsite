Felony Battery in Florida: Penalties Under F.S. 784.041

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | April 8, 2026
Criminal Defense Battery Charges Felony Penalties

Most battery arrests in Florida start as misdemeanors. But two things can turn an ordinary altercation into a third-degree felony carrying up to five years in state prison: how badly the other person was hurt, or whether you've been convicted of battery before. As a former Florida Highway Patrol trooper and deputy sheriff, I've seen how quickly that line gets crossed — often before the person arrested understands what changed.

What Makes Battery a Felony in Florida

Florida's felony battery statute, F.S. 784.041, sits between two charges you may already know. Below it is simple battery under F.S. 784.03 — a first-degree misdemeanor for intentionally touching or striking someone against their will. Above it is aggravated battery under F.S. 784.045 — a second-degree felony involving a deadly weapon, an intent to cause great bodily harm, or a pregnant victim. Felony battery is the middle rung: a simple battery that causes great bodily harm, permanent disability, or permanent disfigurement, even if the person never intended that result and never used a weapon. The same statute also covers domestic battery by strangulation in a separate subsection. For a broader map of how these charges relate, see our overview of battery charges in Florida and their degrees and defenses.

Here's the part that surprises people: the touching itself can be minimal. The Florida Supreme Court noted in State v. Hearns that battery can be committed by any intentional touching, however slight. What elevates the charge isn't the force you used — it's the injury that resulted, or the record you carry.

The Two Paths to a Felony Charge

Path one is the injury. The statute requires great bodily harm, permanent disability, or permanent disfigurement — and Florida courts have drawn that line in real cases. In Coronado v. State, the court held that "great bodily harm" means great as distinguished from slight, trivial, minor, or moderate harm, and that ordinary bruises from a simple battery don't qualify. On the other side of the line, Heck v. State found an orbital (eye socket) fracture with swelling sufficient. That's the practical takeaway: a bare-handed fight — no weapon at all — that ends with a fractured eye socket or a broken jaw is routinely charged under this prong. Fists plus a fracture is a felony.

Path two is your record. Under F.S. 784.03(2), a person with even one prior conviction for battery, aggravated battery, or felony battery who commits any new battery — no serious injury required — faces a third-degree felony. And "conviction" is broader than most people think: it includes withheld adjudications and no-contest pleas.

New for 2026: The Prior-Conviction Path Just Got Wider

The Florida Legislature passed SB 436 this session — nearly unanimously, 37-0 in the Senate and 110-1 in the House — and it takes effect July 1, 2026. The bill adds resisting an officer with violence to the list of priors that elevate a new battery to a felony, and makes battery on a law enforcement officer causing bodily injury a prison-releasee-reoffender qualifying offense, which can mean mandatory-minimum exposure for someone recently released from prison.

Penalties for a Third-Degree Felony

Felony battery is punishable by up to 5 years in state prison under F.S. 775.082, up to 5 years of probation, and a fine of up to $5,000 under F.S. 775.083. But the maximums only tell half the story. Under Florida's Criminal Punishment Code, felony battery is ranked at Level 6 on the offense severity chart in F.S. 921.0022 — a mid-high ranking that generates substantial sentencing points on its own. Add victim-injury points, which are nearly automatic in a great-bodily-harm case, or points from a prior record, and the scoresheet can make prison the lowest permissible sentence rather than a worst-case outcome. Our guide to the Florida criminal punishment scoresheet explains how those points are calculated.

The Consequences That Outlast the Sentence

A felony battery conviction makes you a convicted felon, which means losing your right to possess a firearm under F.S. 790.23 — itself a felony if violated. And felony battery appears on the list of offenses ineligible for record sealing under F.S. 943.0584, so the charge can follow you through background checks for employment and housing indefinitely.

What the State Must Prove — and Where the Defense Pushes Back

On the injury path, the State must prove an intentional touching or striking against the person's will and that it caused great bodily harm, permanent disability, or permanent disfigurement. On the prior-conviction path, the prior isn't just a sentencing detail — the Florida Supreme Court held in State v. Harbaugh that it's an element the State must prove to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt, in a bifurcated proceeding so the jury decides guilt on the new battery before ever hearing about the old one. Sloppy or incomplete certified records of that prior are a real vulnerability in the State's case.

Defense Angles an Attorney Examines

Every felony battery case gets examined from several directions, because weakness at any single element can drop the charge to a misdemeanor — or end it entirely.

Common Defenses to F.S. 784.041 Charges

  • Injury severity: under Coronado, bruises and moderate harm don't meet the felony threshold — medical records often tell a different story than the arrest report.
  • Disputed intent: accidental or incidental contact isn't battery at all.
  • Self-defense and Stand Your Ground: F.S. 776.012 justifies defensive force, and F.S. 776.032 provides immunity that can be raised at a pretrial hearing where the State must disprove self-defense by clear and convincing evidence. Learn more in our explainer on Florida's Stand Your Ground law.
  • Mutual combat: a fight both people willingly joined looks very different to a jury than an attack.
  • Challenging the prior: under Harbaugh, the State must prove the qualifying prior conviction with accurate certified records — and defense counsel checks every one.

What to Do If You're Charged in Orange County

Felony battery cases here usually begin as on-scene probable-cause arrests — often on a domestic or disturbance call — and the responding officer's injury documentation largely determines whether the booking says misdemeanor or felony. I wrote those reports for years: the photos, the EMS transport records, the witness statements. But the arrest isn't the charge. The real decision comes when the State Attorney's Office reviews the case for filing — and charges are routinely upgraded to F.S. 784.041 once hospital records confirm a fracture, or elevated under F.S. 784.03(2) once a records check surfaces a qualifying prior. That pre-filing window is the single highest-leverage period for the defense: it's when an attorney can present the other side of the story before the charging document is drafted. In the meantime, stay silent about the incident, preserve any video or messages, write down witness names, and prepare for your first court appearance in Orlando. Keep in mind that Orange County publishes a daily jail booking report, so an initial felony battery booking is public before any charge is formally filed.
