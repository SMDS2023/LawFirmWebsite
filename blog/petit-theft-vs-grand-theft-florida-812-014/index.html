Petit vs Grand Theft in Florida: The $750 Line

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | September 14, 2026
Theft Charges Shoplifting Felony vs. Misdemeanor

Petit theft versus grand theft in Florida is a question of dollar value. Property worth $750 or more is grand theft, a felony, and anything less is petit theft, a misdemeanor. The number on the store's loss-prevention report decides which courthouse you walk into, and that number can be challenged.

This post walks the tiers in order, then turns to the real fight: how the value gets set. A receipt, a clearance tag, or a rough estimate can move a shoplifting case from one side of the line to the other.

Where do the dollar lines fall under F.S. 812.014?

F.S. 812.014 grades theft by the value of the property taken. Three lines matter for most people: $100, $750, and $20,000.

The theft tiers at a glance

  • Under $100: second-degree misdemeanor petit theft, up to 60 days in jail and a $500 fine.
  • $100 to under $750: first-degree misdemeanor petit theft, up to one year in jail and a $1,000 fine.
  • $750 to under $20,000: third-degree felony grand theft, up to five years in prison and a $5,000 fine.
  • $20,000 to under $100,000: second-degree felony grand theft, up to 15 years in prison.
  • $100,000 or more: first-degree felony grand theft, up to 30 years in prison.

The line that changes your life sits at $750. The Legislature set it there in 2019, reviewed it in 2024, and left it alone. The State has five years to file on either side of that line, a trap covered in the retail theft statute of limitations post.

How does the store set the number?

Value means market value at the time and place of the theft under F.S. 812.012. If market value cannot be determined, replacement cost within a reasonable time is used instead.

The Florida Supreme Court held in Negron v. State that value is an element the State must prove. For used property, purchase price alone is not enough. Age, condition, and depreciation all count, and appellate courts keep reducing grand theft convictions when the State skips that proof.

Shoplifting works differently. In F.T. v. State, the Third District held that the sale price on the tag sets the value in a retail case. That is why the loss-prevention report lists every item at its shelf or point-of-sale price and adds them up.

Why a receipt or clearance tag matters more than you think

In Orange County a shoplifting case usually starts with store staff, not police. Loss prevention watches the concealment, lets you pass the last register, detains you, and calls OPD or the Sheriff's Office. The officer's charging decision rests on the store's item list.

A price-match screenshot or a clearance tag can pull that list under $750. In Blount v. State, the Fourth District reduced a felony retail theft to a misdemeanor because the witness offered rough estimates instead of prices.

Which items are grand theft at any price?

Some property skips the dollar test entirely. The same theft statute makes it a third-degree felony to take a firearm, a motor vehicle, a will, a stop sign, or any amount of a controlled substance. It also reaches farm animals, citrus, construction-site property, and installed fire extinguishers.

Since October 2024, a package worth $40 or more taken from a porch or yard is grand theft as well.

When do several small trips become one felony?

F.S. 812.015 is the retail theft statute, and it stacks on top of the general theft law. Since October 1, 2024, the State can add up separate retail thefts committed within a 120-day window.

  • $750 within 120 days: a third-degree felony if the total is reached by working with others, planning to resell, or stealing from more than one store.
  • Three or more thefts taking 10 or more items: a third-degree felony at two or more stores, regardless of value.
  • Over $3,000 within 120 days, or three thefts taking 20 or more items: a second-degree felony with 15 years of exposure.
  • Booster bags or tag removers: a third-degree felony on their own, no matter what the merchandise is worth.

Thefts spread across more than one judicial circuit go to the Statewide Prosecutor. Two news reports from September 2026 show both ends of the range.

Local reporting on a Jacksonville Beach police investigation described one Ulta store hit three separate times since late 2025, each trip topping $1,000. Every trip clears $750 on its own. If one person were tied to trips inside the same 120-day window, the aggregation rule would let prosecutors add them up.

At the other end, the Attorney General's office announced charges against a baby-formula ring tied to more than 150 thefts across six circuits. Prosecutors filed racketeering and dealing-in-stolen-property counts and pointed to 30-year exposure for repeat felony retail theft.

What happens after a first theft conviction?

Prior convictions move the line for you

One prior theft conviction makes any new petit theft a first-degree misdemeanor. Two or more priors make a new petit theft a third-degree felony, even for a $20 item. A second shoplifting conviction also carries a mandatory fine or court-ordered public service.

A common myth needs correcting. Adults convicted of petit theft in Florida no longer face a driver license suspension for the theft itself. The Legislature removed that penalty in 2019, and the remaining theft suspension applies only to drivers under 18.

Expect a civil demand letter from the store's outside lawyer under F.S. 772.11. It asks for three times the loss, with a $200 floor, plus attorney fees. It is not a court order, and paying it does not settle the criminal case.

Theft is also a crime of dishonesty. The Florida Supreme Court held in State v. Page that even a petit theft conviction can be used to impeach you if you testify. That label follows the conviction onto background checks, which is why sealing or expunging a theft record matters so much.

Why does the $750 fight decide the whole case?

A petit theft charge is heard in county court. A grand theft charge goes to circuit court with a felony scoresheet. On that scoresheet, grand theft between $750 and $5,000 is a Level 2 offense. Felony retail theft under the aggregation rules scores at Level 5 or 6.

Misdemeanor pretrial diversion in the Ninth Circuit generally requires no more than one prior nonviolent misdemeanor conviction. Felony pretrial intervention exists for some nonviolent third-degree felonies under F.S. 948.08, but it needs State Attorney approval and the victim's consent, and the retailer is the victim. Staying on the misdemeanor side keeps the simpler program open.

The valuation fight settles three things at once: the courthouse, the scoresheet, and your best shot at a dismissal.
