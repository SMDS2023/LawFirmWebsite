Petit vs. Grand Theft: Florida's $750 Line

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | April 12, 2026
Theft Crimes Petit Theft Grand Theft

Florida's theft dollar threshold is the price line in F.S. 812.014 that decides whether a theft charge is a misdemeanor or a felony. Property worth $750 or more is grand theft, a third-degree felony, and anything under that is petit theft. For a first Orlando shoplifting stop, that line sets your courthouse, diversion options, and sealing rights.

Where does the $750 line fall?

F.S. 812.014 grades theft by the value of what was taken. The ladder has five main rungs.

  • Under $100: second-degree misdemeanor petit theft, up to 60 days in jail and a $500 fine.
  • $100 to under $750: first-degree misdemeanor petit theft, up to one year in jail and a $1,000 fine.
  • $750 to under $20,000: third-degree felony grand theft, up to five years in prison and a $5,000 fine.
  • $20,000 to under $100,000: second-degree felony.
  • $100,000 or more: first-degree felony.

If the State says your total crosses $750, ask how it got there. Old store visits can also resurface years later, as explained in the five-year retail theft statute of limitations trap.

Why $750?

The Legislature raised the felony line from $300 to $750 effective October 1, 2019. The statute now requires a review of the dollar thresholds every five years, and the 2024 review left the $750 line in place.

How does the State prove what the property was worth?

Value is an element of the crime, so the State must prove it beyond a reasonable doubt. F.S. 812.012 defines value as market value at the time and place of the theft. If market value cannot be found, replacement cost within a reasonable time is the fallback.

In Negron v. State, the Florida Supreme Court said the jury may weigh original cost, how the item was used, its condition, and depreciation. A receipt alone is often not enough. In Lucky v. State, the Fourth District reversed a grand theft conviction built on a six-month-old television's purchase price and testimony that it worked.

What if no one proves value at all? In Marrero v. State, the Florida Supreme Court held that jurors cannot fill the gap with life experience. Without proof, the conviction drops to second-degree petit theft, the lowest rung.

One caveat matters for shoplifting. The retail theft statute defines value as the sale price when the item was taken. A Florida appellate court applied that tag price in F.T. v. State. In a store case, the defense turns on aggregation, identity, and intent. Proof questions return at sentencing, as covered in challenging inflated restitution in Florida criminal cases.

Which items are grand theft at any price?

Some property is a third-degree felony no matter what it is worth. The same statute lists, among others:

  • A firearm or a motor vehicle.
  • A will or other testamentary instrument.
  • A stop sign or a fire extinguisher installed in a building.
  • Any amount of a controlled substance.
  • Property taken from a posted construction site.

The dwelling rule catches the most people. Property worth $40 or more but under $750 taken from a home or its unenclosed curtilage, meaning the yard or porch, is grand theft. A $50 package lifted from a porch is a felony. Since October 1, 2024, theft from more than 20 dwellings is a second-degree felony.

Can the State add up separate store visits?

Yes, and this is how small stops become a felony. F.S. 812.015, the retail theft statute, lets prosecutors aggregate thefts within a 120-day window. Since October 1, 2024, three thefts obtaining ten or more items from two or more locations, or $750 or more in aggregate, is a third-degree felony. Before that change, the window was 30 days.

The second-degree tier covers more than $3,000 in aggregate within 120 days. It also covers three or more thefts in that window taking 20 or more items from two or more stores.

Acting with five or more people to overwhelm a store is a third-degree felony on its own. If the group was recruited through social media, it becomes a second-degree felony. The 2024 law lengthened the aggregation window and added the group and social-media provisions. Carrying a foil-lined bag or tag remover is its own felony regardless of value.

Three small trips, one felony

Three stops at different stores over four months, none crossing $750, can be charged as felony retail theft if the State ties them together. The receipt total from any one visit does not settle the charge.

What does a first petit theft carry beyond the fine?

More than most people expect. Four consequences are worth knowing before you enter a plea.

  • Prior-conviction escalators: one prior theft conviction bumps petit theft to a first-degree misdemeanor. Two or more priors make any petit theft a third-degree felony, whatever the item cost.
  • Driver's license: an adult shoplifting conviction alone does not suspend your license. A petit theft conviction for driving off without paying for gas carries a mandatory suspension, six months for a first and one year after that. For a defendant under 18, the court may suspend the license for six months to a year.
  • Civil demand letters: under F.S. 772.11, a store may demand three times its actual damages, with a $200 minimum, plus attorney's fees. Paying the letter does not close the criminal case, and it is separate from restitution.
  • Employment and immigration: a theft conviction follows you into background checks, and non-citizens should review their status with counsel before any plea.

Why does the line matter at the first hearing?

Because the two sides of the line run through different systems. A first petit theft in Orange County often starts with a notice to appear instead of a booking, and it is heard in county court. A grand theft charge usually means a custodial arrest and a circuit court case scored under the Criminal Punishment Code scoresheet.

Diversion follows the same split. Misdemeanor diversion programs are common for a first petit theft. Felony diversion exists but is narrower.

The record consequence lasts longest. A withhold of adjudication on petit theft can usually be sealed, while a felony theft adjudication cannot. The rules are explained in sealing and expunging a Florida criminal record. That is why the first job in any theft case is testing whether the felony count belongs on the facts.
