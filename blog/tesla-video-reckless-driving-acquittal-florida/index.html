The State Dropped Felony Fleeing. The Judge Threw Out Reckless Driving.

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | October 21, 2026
Case Review Reckless Driving Fleeing and Eluding Judgment of Acquittal Tesla Video Evidence Seminole County

A Winter Springs driver was arrested at gunpoint in his own driveway for felony fleeing. The State dropped that charge before it filed an information. It filed only reckless driving, a misdemeanor. The judge granted a judgment of acquittal on reckless driving before the jury could decide.

The Record

  • The stop: August 7, 2024, in Winter Springs. The officer drew his gun at the driver's garage. The driver was put on his knees and handcuffed.
  • The arrest: felony fleeing and eluding. The State dropped it before filing an information.
  • The charge tried: reckless driving under F.S. 316.192, a misdemeanor.
  • The ruling: on July 16, 2026, Seminole County Judge Debra Krause granted a judgment of acquittal on reckless driving.
  • The tickets: failure to yield and an expired tag were dismissed. The driver pleaded no contest to improper passing, with a withhold and a $300 fine.

This was not a Lotter Law case. Defense attorney Ethan Carlos of Matthews R. Bark, P.A. tried it. Every fact here comes from the public trial transcript unless another source is named.

What Happened at SR 434 and Michael Blake Boulevard?

The driver was heading home after dropping his kids at school. A new traffic signal hung over the intersection, but it was not working yet.

He went around stopped traffic in the left-turn lane and turned onto Michael Blake Boulevard. Officer Erik Schwartz of the Winter Springs Police Department was stopped across the intersection in an unmarked car. He turned on his emergency lights.

The two accounts split from there. The officer testified that the Tesla kept going “at a high rate of speed” through a neighborhood and into a garage. The driver testified that he first heard sirens after his garage door closed. He opened it to see what was going on.

The officer drew his gun. The driver was ordered to his knees and handcuffed. At trial, the officer agreed the driver complied with every command.

What Happened to the Fleeing Charge?

The State dropped it. The officer arrested the driver for felony fleeing, but the State never filed that charge in an information, the formal charging document. The record does not say why. Before trial, the prosecutor told the judge that “he is not charged with fleeing and eluding in this case.”

Fleeing turns on what you knew. Under F.S. 316.1935(1), the State must prove you knew an officer ordered you to stop and you willfully refused. The higher tier in subsection (2) requires a patrol car with agency markings “prominently displayed” and its siren and lights on.

Here the car was unmarked. The driver said he never saw it behind him, and the officer confirmed he said so.

Reckless driving was what remained. F.S. 316.192(1)(b) makes fleeing an officer in a car “reckless driving per se.” That is why the prosecutor called reckless driving a lesser-included offense of fleeing.

What Is the Difference Between Careless and Reckless Driving?

Careless driving is a ticket. Reckless driving is a crime. That gap decided this case.

  • Careless driving, F.S. 316.1925: failing to drive “in a careful and prudent manner.” You are cited for a moving violation under chapter 318.
  • Reckless driving, F.S. 316.192: driving “in willful or wanton disregard for the safety of persons or property.” A first conviction carries up to 90 days in jail, a $25 to $500 fine, or both.

Willful and wanton are hard words to prove. The State has to show you meant it, or that you knew harm was likely and did not care. A bad turn is not enough.

The judge drew that line herself. She said the officer's testimony “would suffice to do a careless driving, but reckless, that is a much more elevated charge than that.”

What Is a Judgment of Acquittal?

It is a ruling that the State's evidence cannot support a conviction. The judge makes it, not the jury. Under Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.380, the court enters it when the evidence is insufficient to warrant a conviction.

The judge reads the evidence in the light most favorable to the State. If the case still falls short, it ends there.

It took two tries here. Defense attorney Ethan Carlos moved for acquittal when the State rested, and Judge Krause denied it. He renewed the motion after the Tesla video came in and the defense rested. This time she granted it.

Then she brought the jurors back and sent them home.

How Did the Tesla Video Matter?

The officer's car had no dash camera. Without the Tesla video, the jury would have had only his memory of a turn he said lasted “10 to 15 seconds at the most.”

He wrote in the citation and in his sworn affidavit that the car “began to fishtail.” The Tesla records to an SD card in the glovebox. After he was released, the driver pulled the card himself and sent the footage to his lawyer.

The raw video came in as Defense Exhibit 1. The State did not object. The defense argued it “affirmatively negates” the officer's observations. The State said it did not. The judge ruled after viewing it.

A Hard-Fought Win for Ethan Carlos

This win was not handed to anyone. The case started as a felony fleeing arrest. The State dropped the felony, but it took reckless driving all the way to trial.

A jury was picked and sworn. The State called its officer and rested. Carlos moved for acquittal, and the judge denied it. So he put on a defense. His client testified, and the Tesla video came in as Defense Exhibit 1. Then Carlos renewed the motion and won it.

He fought the smaller battles too. Before trial, he asked that the officer be kept from telling the jury about the dropped felony. That is the kind of pretrial ruling a motion in limine asks for. The prosecutor agreed to instruct the officer not to mention “potential felonies that never amounted to anything.”

What Can You Take From This Case?

  • Save your own video. This driver pulled the SD card the same day. Without it, the officer's account was the only one.
  • Comply at the scene. He did, and the officer said so under oath. You argue your case in court, not in the driveway.
  • Read the charge, not the arrest. An arrest for “fleeing” became a trial on reckless driving. What the State files is what you defend.
  • Know the gap between a ticket and a crime. Careless driving costs a fine and points. Reckless driving can cost jail.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is reckless driving a felony in Florida?

Usually not. A first conviction is punishable by up to 90 days. It becomes a first-degree misdemeanor if it damages another person's property or person, and a third-degree felony if it causes serious bodily injury, under F.S. 316.192(3).

Can I be charged with fleeing if I never saw the police?

F.S. 316.1935(1) requires that you knew an officer ordered you to stop. Whether you knew is a fact the State has to prove.

Is careless driving a crime?

No. Under F.S. 316.1925(2), it is a moving violation punished under chapter 318.

Can the State drop a charge you were arrested for?

Yes. The arrest charge is the officer's call. The formal charge is the State Attorney's. Prosecutors file formal charges in an information, and they can file less than the arrest charge or nothing at all. Here the arrest was felony fleeing, and the State filed only reckless driving.

What happens after a judgment of acquittal?

The case ends on that charge. The jury is released, and there is no conviction and no sentence. The arrest record remains, but an acquittal may make it eligible for sealing or expungement.
