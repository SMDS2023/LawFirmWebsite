Careless vs Reckless Driving in Florida

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | September 28, 2026
Traffic Offenses Reckless Driving Careless Driving

Careless vs reckless driving in Florida is not a matter of how an officer fills out a ticket. It is two different statutes with two different mental states. One is a crime. The other is a noncriminal moving violation.

Reckless driving under F.S. 316.192 is a deliberate, conscious disregard of a known danger. Careless driving under F.S. 316.1925 is inattention that leads to a mistake. Everything else follows from that difference.

What Is the Real Difference Between Careless and Reckless Driving?

The difference is what was going on in the driver's mind. Reckless driving means the driver knew about a danger to people or property and chose to disregard it. Careless driving means the driver was not paying enough attention, and that led to a mistaken act.

Put another way, the reckless driver sees the risk and drives into it anyway. The careless driver misses the risk, and a mistake follows.

A bad outcome alone does not make driving reckless. A serious crash can come from simple inattention. The question is the mental state behind the driving, not how badly things turned out.

That is why the two offenses sit in different systems. Under F.S. 318.14, careless driving is charged as a noncriminal infraction. Reckless driving is a criminal offense. If you already face the criminal side, read about a first-time reckless driving charge in Florida.

What Does F.S. 316.192 Require for Reckless Driving?

The reckless driving statute is short. Subsection (1)(a) reads:

"Any person who drives any vehicle in willful or wanton disregard for the safety of persons or property is guilty of reckless driving."

Two details matter. First, the statute says "or." The state can rely on willful disregard or on wanton disregard. It does not need to prove both.

Second, the statute protects persons and property. Disregard for another person's car, fence, or storefront falls within its reach, not only disregard for people.

In plain terms, the required mental state is a deliberate, conscious disregard of a known danger to people or property. The driver recognizes the risk and chooses to ignore it. That choice is what makes reckless driving a crime rather than an infraction.

The statute sets the penalties this way:

  • First conviction: imprisonment for not more than 90 days or a fine of not less than $25 nor more than $500, or both.
  • Second or subsequent conviction: imprisonment for not more than 6 months or a fine of not less than $50 nor more than $1,000, or both.
  • Causing damage to the property or person of another: a misdemeanor of the first degree.
  • Causing serious bodily injury to another: a felony of the third degree.

A Reckless Conviction Is a Criminal Conviction

A reckless driving conviction is a criminal conviction. A careless driving citation is a noncriminal moving violation. That difference stays with you long after the case ends.

What Does F.S. 316.1925 Require for Careless Driving?

The careless driving statute looks at how you drove, given the conditions around you. Subsection (1) reads:

"Any person operating a vehicle upon the streets or highways within the state shall drive the same in a careful and prudent manner, having regard for the width, grade, curves, corners, traffic, and all other attendant circumstances, so as not to endanger the life, limb, or property of any person. Failure to drive in such manner shall constitute careless driving and a violation of this section."

Subsection (2) says how it is charged:

"Any person who violates this section shall be cited for a moving violation, punishable as provided in chapter 318."

Put simply, careless driving is driving with an indifference or inattention to the road. That indifference leads to a mistaken act. You misjudge a gap, drift over a line, or look away at the wrong moment.

The statute does not require that you knew about a danger and ignored it. It asks only whether you drove carefully and prudently for the conditions.

Why Does the Mental State Matter to You?

It decides which system handles your case. F.S. 318.17 excludes reckless driving from the chapter that governs noncriminal traffic infractions. Reckless driving cannot be handled like a regular ticket.

Careless driving stays in that noncriminal system. It is a moving violation with a civil penalty. Reckless driving is a criminal case, with possible jail and a criminal conviction.

The mental state is also what the state must prove. For reckless driving, a bad outcome is not enough. The prosecutor must prove willful or wanton disregard beyond a reasonable doubt.

That is where the defense focuses. Did the driver actually know about the danger and consciously disregard it? Or was this inattention that led to a mistake?

Questions That Separate the Two

  • What danger to people or property was actually present?
  • Is there evidence the driver knew about that danger?
  • Did the driver consciously choose to disregard it?
  • Or does the evidence show inattention that led to a mistake?
