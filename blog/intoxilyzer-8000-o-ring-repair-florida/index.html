When an O-Ring Swap Is an Unauthorized Repair

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | August 16, 2026
DUI Defense Breath Tests Florida Case Law

It costs about a dollar. It's a small black rubber ring you could lose in your pocket. And according to a ruling out of Duval County this month, the way Florida has been swapping that little part in and out of the Intoxilyzer 8000 may put breath test results across the state in jeopardy.

One Rubber Ring, Thousands of Cases

As reported by Action News Jax in August 2026, a three-judge panel in Duval County took up a DUI case in which the defense challenged how the Intoxilyzer 8000 — the breath-test machine used throughout Florida — is maintained. The specific issue: when technicians replace the machine's rubber O-rings, is that routine maintenance, or is it a repair? The panel treated O-ring replacement as a repair. That distinction matters enormously, because under Florida's breath-testing rules, repairs may only be performed by an authorized repair facility — and the state conceded that neither the Florida Department of Law Enforcement (FDLE) nor the Jacksonville Sheriff's Office is one.

If that sounds like a technicality, consider what rides on it. Florida's DUI statute, F.S. 316.193, makes it a crime to drive with a breath-alcohol level of 0.08 or above, and the breath number is often the state's most powerful piece of evidence. This machine has a long, contentious history in Florida courtrooms — I've written before about how the Intoxilyzer 8000 became nearly untouchable in Florida litigation — and this ruling strikes at something new: not the software or the science, but the wrench-turning.

Repair vs. Annual Maintenance: Where Florida Draws the Line

Florida's breath-testing program only works legally because of a bargain struck decades ago. Under the implied-consent law, F.S. 316.1932, breath tests must be performed substantially according to methods approved by FDLE, and F.S. 316.1934(5) gives the state a shortcut: a rule-compliant test comes into evidence with a simple affidavit. The Florida Supreme Court blessed this framework in State v. Bender and reaffirmed in Robertson v. State that the state carries the burden of showing substantial compliance with the administrative rules. Lose the compliance, and under State v. Miles the state loses the shortcut — it must prove the science the hard way, if it can.

Those administrative rules, in Chapter 11D-8 of the Florida Administrative Code, distinguish between inspection tasks FDLE performs and repairs that only an authorized facility may handle. The Duval ruling didn't come out of nowhere on this point.

The Doctrinal Predecessor: State v. Kilburn

In August 2025, Florida's Second District Court of Appeal let stand suppression orders in State v. Kilburn and its companion cases, where trial courts found that FDLE inspectors replacing an Intoxilyzer 8000 breath tube was a repair — not maintenance. Because FDLE is not an authorized repair facility, the tests were not in substantial compliance with Chapter 11D-8, and the results were inadmissible under F.S. 316.1934(5). The Duval O-ring ruling extends that same logic to a part that gets replaced far more often.

Four of Five O-Rings, Fleet-Wide

Here's what makes this ruling bigger than one machine in one county. According to the Action News Jax reporting, FDLE's own testimony was that technicians replace four of the five O-rings during annual maintenance — as standard practice. A Jacksonville Sheriff's Office witness admitted the agency replaced O-rings without keeping records of it, and acknowledged the work could affect the machine's accuracy. NewsRadio WIOD's statewide coverage made the same point: because the challenge attacks FDLE's routine maintenance practice rather than one defective unit, it potentially reaches Intoxilyzer 8000 machines used across Florida — including the ones processing breath samples in Orange County.

That's not an argument that any particular machine read high or low. It's an argument that the legal foundation for admitting the number — documented, rule-compliant maintenance — may be missing across the fleet. And the machines' track record is worth scrutiny on its own; when we analyzed 134,000 Florida breath tests, the data raised plenty of its own questions.

What This Means for Your Case File

Think of this like a citizens-academy walkthrough of how a breath number actually reaches a jury. It isn't automatic. The state has to lay a paper foundation, and that paper trail is checkable in every single case. FDLE maintains public records of Intoxilyzer 8000 inspections, so a defense review can trace exactly what was done to the specific machine that tested you — and what wasn't documented.

The Records a Defense Review Examines

The Paper Trail Behind Every Breath Test

  • Annual department inspections: FDLE's yearly inspection of each machine — where, per FDLE's own testimony, most O-rings get swapped.
  • Monthly agency inspections: the local agency's recurring checks between annual inspections.
  • Repair and parts records: what was replaced, by whom, and whether the facility was authorized under Chapter 11D-8 — the exact gap the Jacksonville ruling exposed, where the sheriff's office admitted swapping O-rings with no records at all.
  • Post-repair re-inspection: whether the machine was properly re-inspected before returning to evidentiary use.

When those records don't add up, the vehicle for challenging the number is a motion to suppress — a pretrial hearing where a judge decides whether the breath result ever reaches a jury. If you want the play-by-play of how that works, see our guide to what to expect at a motion to suppress hearing.

A Suppressed Breath Test Is Not an Automatic Win

Be clear-eyed about this: suppression removes the number, not the case. A Florida DUI almost always starts roadside, not at the machine — the driving pattern, the officer's observations, body-camera video, and field sobriety exercises all happen before anyone blows into an Intoxilyzer. As the Jacksonville reporting itself noted, prosecutors can still proceed on that evidence. And the administrative license suspension under F.S. 322.2615 runs on its own short clock regardless. That roadside evidence has its own vulnerabilities — the HGN eye test can be challenged too — but it doesn't disappear with the breath result.

The Bigger Intoxilyzer Picture — and the Timing

For years, the Intoxilyzer 8000 shrugged off attacks. Defendants who sought its source code ran into a wall when the Florida Supreme Court held in Ulloa v. CMI, Inc. that reaching the Kentucky manufacturer's documents required an out-of-state witness procedure most cases never completed. What's different now is that Kilburn and the Duval O-ring ruling target something no manufacturer can shield: the maintenance records sitting in Florida filing cabinets — or, as in Jacksonville, not sitting anywhere at all. One caveat worth stating plainly: this is a trial-level panel ruling reported in the news, not a binding appellate opinion with a published citation, and the state can be expected to fight it.

The timing matters for Central Florida drivers. Florida law enforcement agencies have announced they're joining the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's "Drive Sober or Get Pulled Over" Labor Day mobilization, with high-visibility DUI enforcement running from mid-August through early September 2026. More saturation patrols mean more stops, more arrests, and more breath samples flowing through the very machines whose maintenance is now in question.
