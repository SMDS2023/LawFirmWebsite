Florida DUI Formal Review: The License Hearing and Your Options

After a DUI arrest, DHSMV starts a separate license case. Formal review is the short-deadline hearing on that case. Here is how that hearing works, and what options sit on the ten-day clock.

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | August 19, 2026
Formal Review License Suspension DUI Defense

A DUI arrest in Florida starts two cases at once. One is criminal court. The other is the DHSMV license suspension. I wrote recently about a formal review that turned on the wrong suspension ground on the citation. This is the map of that hearing, not a rewrite of that post.

Most people hear DHSMV and think "the DMV" — the counter for a license or a tag. DHSMV is the parent. Under that roof you have FHP, driver licenses, tags, the BAR office, and investigations. Same department. Different hats. The BAR office is the Bureau of Administrative Reviews, not The Florida Bar.

No Separation of Powers

One body does it all. If a Trooper or a DOT officer arrests you, that same government body can arrest, prosecute, judge, and sentence. The cost is the license. That is not a courtroom with separate roles. You do not beat the structure by calling it illegal. These hearings turn on the technicalities: the ground served, the packet, and the clock.

People Hear BAR Office. They Mean the Wrong BAR.

Clients hear "BAR office" and think of lawyer discipline. That is the wrong BAR. The Florida Bar regulates attorneys. The office that handles a DUI administrative license suspension is DHSMV's Bureau of Administrative Reviews.

A formal review is the short-deadline DHSMV hearing on the administrative suspension that follows a DUI arrest — implied consent, unlawful alcohol level, or both — under section 322.2615, Florida Statutes. It is not the criminal DUI case. It has a different decision-maker, a different burden, and a different set of issues. Winning it does not dismiss the criminal charge. Losing the criminal case does not automatically decide the license case either. The two systems overlap. They are not the same.

The hearing officer is designated by the department. The hearing is typically held at a BAR office, or by communications technology. In Central Florida, the Orlando BAR office is the one that handles Orange County and neighboring counties. That is Orlando in Orange County — not Orange City in Volusia County.

The clock is short. The driver generally has 10 days from the arrest or the issuance of the notice of suspension to request a formal or informal review. DHSMV uses HSMV Form 78065 for that request. Miss the window, and the license issue can be lost before the first criminal court date.

Why the Process Is Lopsided

In criminal court, the State Attorney prosecutes, a judge who does not work for the police department rules on the evidence, and the burden is beyond a reasonable doubt. Formal review is narrower and more stacked.

Start with the Trooper example, because it is the cleanest same-entity illustration. Florida Highway Patrol troopers are DHSMV employees. If an FHP Trooper arrests you for DUI:

  • A DHSMV employee made the arrest and issued the notice of suspension.
  • DHSMV presents the administrative packet and asks the hearing officer to sustain the suspension.
  • A DHSMV hearing officer rules on the evidence and decides the license result.
  • DHSMV writes the hearing rules — including Chapter 15A-6, Florida Administrative Code.

That is the same department at every step. The Legislature set it up that way. I am not saying the hearing is void, or that every suspension should fall. I am saying the process is not a level courtroom, and the defense has to treat it like the agency proceeding it is.

Not every DUI arrest is an FHP arrest. Orange County deputies, Orlando police officers, and other local agencies make these arrests every week. Those officers do not work for DHSMV. The administrative case still does. The packet still goes to the department. The hearing officer is still DHSMV. The rules are still DHSMV's. The Trooper example is just the version where the arresting agency and the reviewing agency wear the same letterhead.

The Hearing Officer's Job Is Narrow

Under section 322.2615, the hearing officer decides whether sufficient cause exists to sustain, amend, or invalidate the administrative suspension. The issues depend on the ground that was served.

If the suspension is for an unlawful breath or blood alcohol level of .08 or higher, the department has to prove that ground — including a qualifying test result. If the suspension is for refusal, the department has to prove a different set of issues: a lawful arrest, a lawful request for a test, a refusal, and the implied-consent warning about the license consequences of refusing.

Those are not the same case. I walked through a real mismatch — citation served the .08 box, the facts were a refusal — in the companion post on the wrong box. The point here is structural: the checkbox on the UTC chooses the playing field, and DHSMV has to prove the field it actually served.

The Rule in Plain English

Formal review is not a mini DUI trial. It is a DHSMV hearing on a short list of statutory issues. The department has to prove the suspension it served, on the timeline the statute gives it, with the record it actually built.

You Win These on Process

People call paperwork problems "technicalities" as if that makes them small. In an agency hearing this compressed, the technicalities are the case. Notice. Service of the citation grounds. Whether the officer had a lawful arrest. Whether the implied-consent warning was given. Whether the packet matches the box that was checked. Whether a subpoenaed officer appears. Whether DHSMV scheduled the hearing in time.

If the department fails to schedule the formal review within 30 days after it receives a timely request, the statute says the department shall invalidate the suspension. Continuances, witness issues, and temporary permits have their own rules. None of that is a closing argument about impairment. It is whether the agency followed the process the Legislature attached to the power it delegated.

What I Look for in the Formal Review File

  • Was the notice of suspension properly issued and served?
  • Which box on the DUI Uniform Traffic Citation was actually checked — .08 or refusal?
  • Does the packet prove the ground that was served, or a different theory?
  • Was there a lawful arrest, and does the record support it?
  • If it is a refusal case, was the implied-consent warning handled the way the statute requires?
  • Were the right officers and witnesses subpoenaed, and did they appear?
  • Did DHSMV meet the request window and the scheduling timeline?

That list is educational, not a promise. Every packet is different. Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome. If a suspension is sustained, a hardship or business-purpose license can be a later option — that is a conversation by phone, not a form on this page.

Two Cases, One Arrest

I tell people the same thing I used to hear from the other side of the glass: the roadside arrest starts the criminal DUI case, but the license issue is already moving on a separate track. The prosecutor, the judge, and the jury are not in the BAR office. The hearing officer is not in county court. A clean formal-review file can still leave a serious criminal case. A weak criminal case can still leave a sustained suspension if the 10-day request was missed.
