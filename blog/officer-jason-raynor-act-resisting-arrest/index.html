Florida's Officer Jason Raynor Act: What It Means for Your Right to Resist an Unlawful Arrest

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | January 30, 2026
New Legislation Resisting Arrest Constitutional Rights

For over a century, Florida law has allowed citizens to resist an unlawful arrest without using force. That right may be about to disappear. The Officer Jason Raynor Act (HB 17/SB 156) is moving through the Florida Legislature, and if passed, it will make resisting any arrest a crime - even if the arrest itself is illegal.

What the Law Currently Says

Under current Florida law (F.S. 843.02), you can only be charged with resisting without violence if you resist an officer engaged in the "lawful execution of any legal duty." This means that if an officer arrests you without probable cause - an unlawful arrest - you have the legal right to verbally refuse, walk away, or otherwise resist without force.

Florida courts have consistently interpreted this to mean that the lawfulness of the arrest matters. If the arrest is illegal, resisting it (without violence) is not a crime.

Current Law: F.S. 843.02

"Whoever shall resist, obstruct, or oppose any officer... in the lawful execution of any legal duty, without offering or doing violence to the person of the officer, shall be guilty of a misdemeanor of the first degree..."

What the Officer Jason Raynor Act Changes

The proposed legislation makes several fundamental changes to Florida's resisting arrest laws:

  • Removes the "lawful" requirement: The bill changes "lawful execution of any legal duty" to simply "official duties." This means you can be charged with resisting even if the arrest is later found to be unlawful.
  • Creates a "good faith" standard: Officers only need to be "acting in good faith" - defined as making "sincere and reasonable efforts to comply with legal requirements, even if the arrest, detention, or other act is later found to have been unlawful."
  • Repeals officer accountability provision: Current law says officers aren't justified in using force if they know the arrest is unlawful. This bill removes that protection.

The Critical Shift

Under the new law, even if an officer arrests you without any legal basis whatsoever, you would be committing a crime by verbally refusing or walking away - unless you can prove the officer was acting in bad faith. That's an extremely high bar.

What Does "Good Faith" Actually Mean?

The bill defines "acting in good faith" as making "sincere and reasonable efforts to comply with legal requirements." Here's the problem: this standard is almost always met.

Consider this scenario: An officer pulls you over because you "look suspicious." They have no traffic violation, no reasonable suspicion of a crime - just a hunch. They order you out of the car and arrest you for "obstruction" when you ask why you're being detained.

  • Under current law: The arrest is unlawful (no probable cause), so resisting it without violence is not a crime.
  • Under the new law: As long as the officer believed they were doing their job correctly - even if they were completely wrong about the law - you're guilty of resisting.

Proving an officer acted in "bad faith" requires showing they knew they were acting unlawfully and did it anyway. That's nearly impossible to prove without body camera footage showing the officer explicitly acknowledging the illegality.

Why Is This Bill Being Proposed?

The bill is named after Officer Jason Raynor, a 26-year-old Daytona Beach police officer who was fatally shot in June 2021. The suspect, Othal Wallace, shot Raynor during a brief encounter behind an apartment complex. Wallace's defense team challenged whether Raynor had acted in "good faith" when he approached Wallace.

This is the third attempt to pass this legislation. The bill is sponsored by Rep. Jessica Baker (R-Jacksonville) in the House and Sen. Tom Leek (R-St. Augustine) in the Senate. The Senate version passed 31-4 in January 2026 and is now before the House.

Bill Status (as of February 2026)

  • SB 156: Passed Florida Senate 31-4 on January 29, 2026
  • HB 17: On House Second Reading Calendar
  • Effective Date: October 1, 2026 (if passed)

What Rights Do You Still Have?

The bill does preserve one critical protection: you can still defend yourself against excessive force. Florida courts have consistently held that if an officer uses unreasonable force - even during a lawful arrest - you have the right to defend yourself proportionally.

However, this creates a dangerous gray area. You cannot resist the arrest itself, but you can resist excessive force during the arrest. In practice, officers and prosecutors often blur these lines.

  • Still legal: Using reasonable force to stop an officer from choking you or breaking your arm
  • Now illegal (under the new law): Verbally refusing to comply with an unlawful order, walking away from an unlawful detention, or pulling your arm back during an illegal arrest

The Concerns Being Raised

The Florida Senate Black Caucus raised concerns about this bill, arguing it could give officers "unchecked authority" in communities of color. In response, sponsors added the "good faith" language - but critics argue this doesn't provide meaningful protection.

Sen. Carlos G. Smith (D-Orlando) was the sole "no" vote in committee, though he didn't publicly explain his opposition. The bill passed the full Senate with only four dissenting votes.

The Practical Effect

If this bill passes, the safest legal advice becomes: comply first, challenge later. Even if you believe an arrest is completely unlawful, your remedy will be in the courtroom - not on the street. Resisting in any way, even verbally, could result in additional criminal charges.

Other Changes in the Bill

The Officer Jason Raynor Act doesn't just change resisting arrest laws. It also:

  • Mandates life without parole for anyone convicted of manslaughter of a law enforcement officer
  • Increases penalties for battery, aggravated battery, and assault against officers
  • Expands protected personnel to include correctional officers, probation officers, and auxiliary officers

What This Means for You

If you're stopped by police in Florida after October 1, 2026, and this bill becomes law, your legal options narrow significantly:

  • Comply with commands - even if you believe they're unlawful
  • Don't argue or debate - verbal resistance could become obstruction
  • Don't walk away - even from an illegal detention
  • Assert your rights calmly - "I do not consent to this search" is still legal
  • Document everything - your defense will happen in court, not on the scene

Charged with Resisting Arrest?

Whether under current law or the new provisions, resisting arrest charges often have viable defenses - especially when the underlying arrest or detention was unlawful. The legality of the officer's actions matters.
