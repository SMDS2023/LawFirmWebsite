When Can Person B Challenge an Illegal Stop of Person A?

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | July 15, 2026
Fourth Amendment Legal Guide Motion to Suppress

Police unlawfully stop Person A. During that detention, officers learn about Person B, find B, and obtain evidence against B. Is everything that follows automatically “fruit of the poisonous tree”? Usually not. The first question is not simply what caused the discovery. It is whose Fourth Amendment rights police violated.

That distinction matters in every motion to suppress evidence. Person B generally cannot borrow Person A's constitutional violation. But B may have a claim if the same stop also seized B, or if officers later searched, detained, or arrested B unlawfully.

The short answer

An unlawful detention of A does not automatically suppress evidence against B. B must connect the challenged evidence to a violation of B's own protected person, home, property, or freedom of movement. Even then, the court must decide whether the evidence remained tainted or whether the connection was legally broken.

Fourth Amendment Rights Are Personal

Lawyers often call this issue “standing.” The Supreme Court's more precise formulation asks whether the challenged police conduct infringed an interest of the particular defendant that the Fourth Amendment protects. In Rakas v. Illinois, the Court rejected the idea that a person can suppress evidence merely because police targeted that person or because the evidence is damaging.

Wong Sun v. United States provides a remarkably close example. Agents unlawfully arrested Toy, obtained information from him, and followed it to narcotics held by someone else. The Court excluded the narcotics against Toy because his unlawful arrest produced them. But the same narcotics were not excluded against Wong Sun because their seizure invaded no protected privacy interest of Wong Sun.

United States v. Payner makes the rule even clearer. Investigators arranged an unlawful search of a third party's briefcase and used the documents to build a case against Payner. The Supreme Court did not condone the misconduct, but it held that Payner could not suppress the evidence because the search did not violate Payner's Fourth Amendment rights.

When Person B May Have a Suppression Claim

B was a passenger in the stopped car

A passenger is not merely a bystander to a traffic stop. In Brendlin v. California, the Supreme Court held that a traffic stop seizes every occupant of a private vehicle. A reasonable passenger would not feel free to leave without police permission. If driver A's car was stopped unlawfully while passenger B remained inside, B can challenge the stop as a violation of B's own freedom.

The stop and the search must still be analyzed separately. A passenger may challenge the seizure caused by the stop but may lack a privacy interest in the driver's glove compartment or another area of someone else's car. The evidence may nevertheless be suppressible if the State obtained it by exploiting B's unlawful seizure. This is one reason the sequence of a Florida traffic stop matters.

Police later violate B's own rights

Suppose the unlawful detention of A supplies B's name or location. Officers then enter B's home without a valid warrant, consent, or recognized exception. B can challenge that entry because the home search implicates B's own privacy. The same is true if officers lack lawful grounds when they later detain B. A defense attorney should examine both links rather than assuming that A's illegality either wins or loses the entire issue.

The encounter actually restrained B

Labels in the report do not control. An officer may describe B as a “witness” or say the contact was “consensual,” but the real question is whether a reasonable person would have felt free to decline the officer's requests or end the encounter. Body-camera video, officer positioning, commands, emergency lights, retained identification, handcuffs, and the duration of the encounter can all matter.

Why “But For the Illegal Stop” Is Not Enough

Fruit of the poisonous tree is not a rule that erases every event after police make a mistake. Wong Sun rejected a pure “but for” test. Courts ask whether police exploited the illegality or instead obtained the evidence through a sufficiently distinguishable route.

Florida's Supreme Court applied that analysis in State v. Frierson. After an unlawful stop, an officer discovered a pre-existing arrest warrant and found a firearm during the search incident to arrest. On those facts, the warrant was an intervening circumstance that sufficiently weakened the connection between the stop and the firearm. The decision does not mean every warrant automatically cures every bad stop. Timing, intervening events, and the purpose and flagrancy of police conduct remain important.

Other issues can include independent source, inevitable discovery, consent, and whether officers already had lawful probable cause before the challenged conduct. A finding that B's rights were violated begins the exclusion analysis; it does not guarantee the result.

Questions the defense should map

  • Who was physically present when the officer made the stop?
  • When did B become unable to leave or end the encounter?
  • Exactly what information came from A, and how was it obtained?
  • Did officers search B, B's property, or B's home?
  • Did a warrant, consent, or independent investigation intervene?
  • What do the body camera, dispatch log, warrant records, and report timestamps show?

The Rule in Florida

Article I, section 12 of the Florida Constitution directs Florida courts to construe the state's search-and-seizure protection in conformity with U.S. Supreme Court Fourth Amendment decisions. The personal-rights rule from Wong Sun, Rakas, Payner, and Brendlin therefore supplies the framework, while Florida cases such as Frierson address how that framework applies to particular facts.

The practical lesson is simple: do not stop the analysis at “the police illegally detained someone.” Identify every person seized, every place searched, the precise route to each item of evidence, and every possible break in that route. Search-and-seizure cases often turn on minutes—or seconds—that a police report compresses into one paragraph.
