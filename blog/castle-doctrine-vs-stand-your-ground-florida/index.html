Florida Castle Doctrine vs. Stand Your Ground

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | September 2, 2026
Self-Defense Castle Doctrine Stand Your Ground

When a string of wrong-address shootings made national news in 2023, a CBS News explainer pointed to the confusion underneath each one: many people assume anyone who steps onto their property can lawfully be met with force. Florida law does not say that. The castle doctrine and stand your ground are two separate rules in the same statute chapter, and the stronger one switches on only in a narrow set of circumstances.

Two Doctrines, One Statute Chapter

Both rules live in Chapter 776 of the Florida Statutes, which is why they get blended together in conversation. F.S. 776.013 is the castle doctrine. It applies inside a dwelling, residence, or occupied vehicle and gives the occupant a legal presumption about their state of mind. F.S. 776.012 is the no-duty-to-retreat rule most people call stand your ground. It applies anywhere a person has a right to be, but it presumes nothing. For the broader history and the 2017 changes, start with our overview of Florida's stand your ground law. This post focuses on the line between the two.

The Castle Doctrine: Where "Home" Ends

F.S. 776.013 does two things. First, subsection (1) says a person in a dwelling or residence where they have a right to be has no duty to retreat. Second, subsection (2) creates a presumption that also reaches an occupied vehicle. If someone was unlawfully and forcibly entering, or had already entered, that dwelling, residence, or occupied vehicle, or was trying to remove a person from it against their will, the law presumes the occupant held a reasonable fear of imminent death or great bodily harm.

Subsection (4) adds a second presumption aimed at the intruder: a person who unlawfully and forcibly enters is presumed to intend an unlawful act involving force or violence. Together, these presumptions do most of the work in a home-defense case.

What Counts as a "Castle" Under F.S. 776.013(5)

A dwelling is any building or conveyance with a roof, mobile or not, designed for people to lodge in at night, and it includes an attached porch. A residence is a dwelling where the person lives or is staying as an invited guest. A vehicle is any conveyance, motorized or not, designed to transport people or property. The driveway, the lawn, and the sidewalk are not on that list.

Stand Your Ground: Anywhere You Have a Right to Be

F.S. 776.012 covers force used outside the castle. Subsection (1) allows nondeadly force when a person reasonably believes it is necessary against imminent unlawful force, with no duty to retreat. Subsection (2) allows deadly force when a person reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent imminent death, great bodily harm, or a forcible felony. The right to stand one's ground under subsection (2) carries conditions:

  • Lawfully present: The person must be in a place where he or she has a right to be. A parking lot, a public sidewalk, or a friend's living room qualifies. A place the person has been ordered to leave does not.
  • Not engaged in criminal activity: The Legislature added this condition in 2014 after appellate courts, in cases like Little v. State and State v. Wonder, noted the older version of F.S. 776.012 had no such limit. A person carrying a firearm they are barred from possessing cannot claim the rule.
  • Imminent: The threat must exist at the moment force is used. Central Florida judges have denied immunity where the danger had already passed.

Presumption vs. Reasonableness: The Key Difference

Location matters, but the real distinction is what each rule does to the question of fear. Inside a dwelling after a forcible entry, F.S. 776.013 presumes the fear was reasonable. Outside it, F.S. 776.012 requires the belief to actually be reasonable on the facts a court later reviews. That is why a confrontation in an Orange County parking lot and one at a front door in the same neighborhood can end very differently. One starts with a presumption on the occupant's side. The other starts from a blank slate, and what the person saw, heard, and knew becomes the whole case.

The Property Line Is Not the Castle Line

Being on someone's land does not trigger the F.S. 776.013 presumption. The statute requires unlawful and forcible entry into the dwelling, residence, or occupied vehicle itself. A stranger in the driveway, at the mailbox, or ringing the doorbell has not forcibly entered anything. Force used against that person is judged under the reasonableness standard of F.S. 776.012.

When the Castle Presumption Does Not Apply

F.S. 776.013(3) lists situations where the presumption switches off even inside a home or vehicle. In those cases, the occupant is back to proving a reasonable belief under the general rule.

Exceptions Under F.S. 776.013(3)

  • The person force was used against had a lawful right to be there, such as a co-owner or lessee, unless a domestic violence injunction or no-contact order barred them.
  • The person was a parent, grandparent, or lawful custodian removing a child in their care.
  • The person using force was engaged in criminal activity or was using the dwelling or vehicle to further criminal activity.
  • The person entering was a law enforcement officer performing official duties who identified themselves or was reasonably identifiable as an officer. F.S. 776.051 separately bars using force to resist arrest by a known officer.

A separate provision, F.S. 776.041, cuts across both doctrines. A person who initially provoked the confrontation, or who was committing or escaping a forcible felony, loses justification under either section unless they withdrew in good faith and clearly communicated it, or faced imminent death with no reasonable means of escape.

How Either Doctrine Reaches a Courtroom

Neither rule stops an investigation. Officers still respond, take statements, seize weapons, and document the scene. F.S. 776.032(2) only says an arrest requires probable cause that the force was unlawful, a standard we explain in our post on probable cause in Florida criminal cases. In practice, Orange and Seminole County agencies often make the arrest or forward the case to the State Attorney, who decides whether to file.

If charges are filed, the defense raises immunity by motion under F.S. 776.032, and the judge decides it at a pretrial evidentiary hearing. That procedure comes from Peterson v. State and Dennis v. State. Since the 2017 amendment to subsection (4), once the defense presents a prima facie claim of self-defense, the State must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the force was not justified. Recent Central Florida rulings on these motions have turned largely on whether the threat was still imminent when force was used. A denial can be challenged by petition for writ of prohibition, and the self-defense claim can still go to the jury at trial. For why this hearing carries more weight than an ordinary motion to dismiss, see our comparison of the stand your ground hearing and the C4 motion.
