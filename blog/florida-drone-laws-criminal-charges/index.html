More Drones, More Questions About Florida Law

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | September 20, 2026
Legal Guide Drone Law Florida Statutes

Florida drone laws matter even when your flight complies with FAA rules. Permission to use the airspace does not automatically authorize everything you do with a drone.

That distinction will become more important as drone services expand. Wing now lists Orlando among its Walmart delivery markets, bringing commercial flights into more Central Florida neighborhoods. Wing’s service information

More flights create more opportunities for mistakes, disputes, and potentially violations of state law. But understanding those laws also means understanding their limits.

Who regulates the flight?

Federal law gives the United States authority over its airspace. The FAA manages airspace use and aviation safety under 49 U.S.C. § 40103.

Florida still has a role. The FAA recognizes room for state laws addressing privacy, harassment, property damage, and other conduct involving drones. Those laws must respect federal authority. FAA guidance

Think of two separate questions: Can you fly there? And what are you doing while you’re there?

Can Florida fill the gaps?

Yes, within limits. Florida can address conduct outside the fields reserved to federal regulation.

The legal principle is called federal preemption. Federal law can displace a state restriction that intrudes into federal authority or conflicts with federal requirements.

The FAA’s guidance identifies aviation safety and airspace efficiency as federally occupied fields. A missing federal rule does not automatically give Florida permission to create its own.

That distinction matters when a state law restricts where drones may fly. Calling the restriction a criminal offense does not resolve the preemption question. FAA preemption guidance

What does Florida prohibit?

F.S. 330.41 contains restrictions involving critical infrastructure, schools, agricultural lands, and certain conduct affecting private property.

The critical-infrastructure provisions address knowing or willful overflight, contact, and interference. A qualifying violation is a third-degree felony. Definitions, property markings, and exceptions matter.

For example, the overflight provision excepts commercial operations authorized by and complying with FAA regulations. Other exceptions also apply.

The same statute requires interpretation consistent with federal law and FAA guidance. The relationship between federal and state authority is therefore part of the analysis.

A drone flying nearby does not automatically establish a crime. The particular location, conduct, intent, and applicable exceptions must be considered.

What about recording someone’s backyard?

Florida’s drone-surveillance law, F.S. 934.50, addresses recording private property or people there with surveillance intent, violating a reasonable expectation of privacy, and lacking written consent.

The statute includes exceptions. Every aerial photograph is not a criminal offense.

Suppose someone deliberately uses a drone to record a person behind a backyard privacy fence. The law presumes privacy when that person cannot be observed from a lawful ground-level vantage point. Visibility from the drone does not erase that protection.

For covered individuals, a knowing or willful violation can be a misdemeanor. Intentionally distributing unlawfully obtained surveillance can make it a felony.

For now, our data supports education rather than claims of widespread prosecution.

Why learn these rules now?

As drone use expands, more pilots and neighbors will encounter these boundaries. More violations are possible; an increase in prosecutions remains to be seen.

Understanding both federal authority and Florida’s restrictions can help people recognize a problem before a flight becomes a dispute.
