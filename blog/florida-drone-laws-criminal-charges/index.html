Florida Drone Laws: FAA Rules vs. State Crimes

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | September 16, 2026
Criminal Defense Drone Law Florida Statutes

Florida drone criminal charges come from state statutes, not from the FAA. The FAA controls the airspace over Orlando, but Florida law controls what you can be arrested for while flying in it.

That split decides who contacts you, where the case is heard, and what you stand to lose. Here is how to tell a federal letter from an Orange County charge.

Who actually owns the sky over Orlando?

The FAA does. Registration, the Part 107 certificate, the 400-foot ceiling, night lighting, and the restricted airspace around Orlando International are all federal rules. Cities cannot regulate flight itself, and Florida wrote that principle into state law.

Delivery drones show the federal layer at work. DroneXL and WFTV report that Wing and Walmart began Part 135 delivery flights from Apopka and Clermont this summer. Those FAA exemptions protect the carrier, not the hobbyist sharing the same sky.

An FAA problem is a civil problem. It arrives weeks later as a letter of investigation, a proposed penalty, or a certificate action. Nobody is arrested for a Part 107 violation. Arrests come from the state layer described next.

The same split will govern flying cars in Lakeland

Flying Magazine reports that a single-seat electric aircraft now carries customers at Lakeland Linder under FAA ultralight rules. Florida's 2026 Advanced Air Mobility Act funds vertiports, but the FAA still certifies the aircraft. Federal airspace, state conduct, same as drones.

What does F.S. 330.41 make a crime?

F.S. 330.41 is the Unmanned Aircraft Systems Act, and it does two jobs. First, it reserves drone regulation to the state. Orlando and Orange County cannot write their own altitude, flight-path, or permit rules, though nuisance and trespass ordinances still apply.

Second, it makes it a crime to knowingly or willfully fly over a critical infrastructure facility, touch it, or disturb its operations. The facility must be fenced or posted, except seaports. The list covers power substations, water plants, cell towers, airports, military sites, state prisons, and county jails.

Since October 1, 2025, that overflight is a third-degree felony, punishable by up to five years in prison and a $5,000 fine. Before that date it was a second-degree misdemeanor. We covered the broader batch of changes in our post on Florida's new laws for July and October 2025.

Schools get their own rule. Flying over a K-12 school without written consent is a second-degree misdemeanor, and recording video raises it to first-degree. Prison and jail overflights, often tied to contraband drops, are the most common felony arrests.

What other Florida drone charges exist?

Companion offenses from the October 2025 law, felony tiers first

  • Weaponized or Remote ID-defeated drone: Flying with a weapon or explosive attached is a third-degree felony. So is disabling the FAA Remote ID broadcast to sneak over critical infrastructure.
  • Careless or intoxicated operation: Florida's aircraft statute makes reckless or drunk flying that endangers others a third-degree felony. PetaPixel reported that St. Petersburg police used it in May 2026 against a pilot buzzing an open car window.
  • Drone surveillance of a home: Knowingly recording private property to invade a reasonable expectation of privacy is a first-degree misdemeanor. Sharing the footage makes it a third-degree felony.
  • Video voyeurism: A drone camera is just an "imaging device" under the voyeurism statute. Secretly recording someone undressing is a third-degree felony for adults.
  • Stalking and contraband: Repeated overflights of a home supported a stalking injunction in a 2022 Fourth District decision. Prison drops add a contraband felony.

The surveillance rule deserves a closer look. F.S. 934.50 presumes a reasonable expectation of privacy anywhere not observable from ground level. Visibility from the air does not defeat that presumption. It fits the stronger privacy tradition we described in our post on Florida's privacy right beyond Miranda.

FAA letter or Florida charge? How to tell what you received

Who contacts you and where it is decided
FAA administrative matter

A letter of investigation or DETER settlement offer from the FAA, usually weeks after the flight; handled in the FAA administrative process with appeal to the NTSB unless waived by a DETER settlement

Florida criminal case

A police officer or deputy on scene, a notice to appear, or an arrest and first appearance; decided in Orange County court, county court for misdemeanors and circuit court for felonies

What it is about and the law involved
FAA administrative matter

Flight rules under 14 C.F.R. Parts 107, 135, and 103 and FAA policy: no Part 107 certificate, no registration, above 400 feet, night flight without lights, a restricted-airspace bust

Florida criminal case

Conduct under F.S. 330.41, 330.411, 934.50, 810.145, 860.13, 784.048, and contraband law 944.47: flying over a fenced plant, substation, airport, or jail; a school; a weapon or disabled Remote ID; filming into a home; reckless or intoxicated operation

What you face
FAA administrative matter

Civil penalty, certificate action, or a reduced-penalty settlement if you admit liability within 10 days

Florida criminal case

Misdemeanor or felony record, jail or prison exposure, fines, probation, and forfeiture of the aircraft

Watch out for and when a defense lawyer helps
FAA administrative matter

A DETER admission of liability can be used against you in a pending state case; a lawyer is usually needed only if a state case is pending or a certificate is at stake

Florida criminal case

An Orlando city drone-permit citation may be preempted by state law under 330.41(3); get a lawyer from the first contact for elements, knowledge, boundary questions, and suppression of police drone video

What does the State have to prove?

No Florida appellate court has interpreted the overflight section or the intent element of the surveillance rule. The elements come from the statutory text, and text is where a defense starts.

For an overflight charge, the State must prove five things. You operated the drone. You did so knowingly or willfully. It was over the facility, touched it, or disturbed operations. The facility was fenced or posted. No exemption applied, such as the owner's written consent.

Boundary questions matter more than altitude. The statute says nothing about height, so position over the fence line is what counts. Knowledge matters too. Did you know a substation sat under your flight path, or did the app show open airspace?

"The FAA said I could fly there" is not a defense. An airspace authorization answers a safety question about the sky. The state statute asks a conduct question about the fence below you. Only a commercial operator complying with FAA rules gets the exception.

Police drone video has its own rules

DroneXL reported in February 2026 that Orlando approved a drone-as-first-responder program, so police drones now reach scenes routinely. The surveillance statute limits drone evidence to warrants, true emergencies, large crowds, and crash scenes. Video gathered outside those limits is inadmissible, a suppression hook like the one in our Flock camera ticket post.

Federal letter or Florida citation? How to tell which you received

Drone cases in Orange County almost never start with the FAA. They start with a complaint from a neighbor, a facility guard, or a jail perimeter patrol. The officer on scene decides the forum.

  • Who contacted you: A deputy, a notice to appear, or an arrest means a state case in Orange County court. A letter from the FAA weeks later means an administrative matter.
  • What it is about: No certificate, no registration, or too high is FAA business. Flying over a fence, a school, a home, or a jail is Florida business.
  • What you face: The FAA can fine you or pull a certificate. Florida can give you a criminal record, jail, probation, and forfeiture of the aircraft.

Since April 2026 the FAA has offered first-time violators a fast-track settlement called DETER, according to its announcement and Federal Register notice. You get ten days to accept a reduced penalty. In exchange you admit liability and waive your appeal.

That admission is administrative, but it does not stay in the FAA's file. If Orange County prosecutors have a parallel case, a signed admission that you flew the drone can surface there. Do not sign a DETER offer while a state charge is pending without talking to a lawyer.

Orlando once had its own drone rule. The city passed a 500-foot ordinance in January 2017, then repealed it that July after F.S. 330.41 took the power away. Today there is no city drone rule to cite you under. State law is the whole game.
