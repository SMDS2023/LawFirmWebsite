Police Liability in Florida: Why Mistakes Aren't Enough

February 1, 2025 | Updated July 13, 2026 | By Jeff Lotter, Esq.

When criminal charges are dismissed after a questionable stop, a flawed report, suppressed evidence, or what someone calls a “technicality,” clients often ask: Can I sue the officer? Sometimes the facts deserve a close civil-rights review. But dismissal ends the prosecution; it does not automatically establish that an officer committed a civil wrong.

Before becoming a lawyer, I spent 13 years as a Florida State Trooper. During that time, I was not aware of anyone in my close professional circle being sued individually over an ordinary mistake made on duty. That is personal experience, not a statistical claim, and it does not mean officers cannot be sued. It illustrates the distance between a mistake that hurts a criminal case and conduct that supports a viable claim for damages.

I do not practice civil police-liability law; Lotter Law is a criminal-defense firm. This article offers a practical overview of why these cases are difficult, what usually is not enough, and what facts may justify prompt review by a lawyer who handles civil-rights litigation.

Dismissal Is Not a Civil Judgment

Criminal and civil cases answer different questions. An officer may need only probable cause to make an arrest, while the State must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt to obtain a conviction. A prosecutor may dismiss because a witness is unavailable, evidence was suppressed, the proof weakened, or continuing the case no longer serves the public interest. None of those outcomes necessarily means the original arrest lacked probable cause.

A successful motion to suppress may reveal an unlawful stop, search, or seizure. That can be important, but a later damages case still requires an actionable legal violation, a proper defendant, causation, provable loss, and compliance with separate deadlines.

Even favorable termination is only one part of a malicious-prosecution theory. Under federal law, ending without a conviction may satisfy that part of the claim, but the plaintiff must still prove the remaining elements and overcome any immunity. Florida malicious-prosecution law has its own requirements, and the reason and circumstances of the dismissal matter. A purely procedural ending or genuine compromise should not be treated as automatically establishing a claim.

Qualified Immunity and Florida Sovereign Immunity

Qualified immunity applies to federal damages claims

42 U.S.C. § 1983 permits claims against a person who, acting under state authority, violates a federal right. It is not a general remedy for careless police work. An officer sued individually may assert qualified immunity. The plaintiff generally must show both a constitutional violation and that the right was clearly established at the time, often through precedent giving reasonable officers fair notice that the particular conduct was unlawful.

That is why poor judgment, negligence, or a department-policy violation may not be enough. Courts evaluate what the officer knew when the decision was made—not simply how the criminal case ended months later.

Florida law follows a separate path

F.S. 768.28 is a limited waiver of Florida’s sovereign immunity for some state-law tort claims. For ordinary conduct within the scope of employment, subsection (9)(a) generally directs the claim against the governmental entity rather than the officer personally. Personal exposure may arise when an officer acted outside the scope of employment or acted in bad faith, with malicious purpose, or with wanton and willful disregard of human rights, safety, or property. The governmental entity generally is not liable for conduct falling within that personal-exposure exception.

The defendant matters

Qualified immunity and Florida sovereign immunity are different doctrines. An individual officer, municipality, sheriff, and state agency are not interchangeable defendants, and a department is not automatically liable merely because it employed the officer.

What Usually Is Not Enough—and What May Matter

These are screening considerations, not guarantees. A civil police-liability attorney must review the complete record.

Usually not enough by itself

  • Dismissal, reduction, acquittal, or a decision not to file charges.
  • The State’s inability to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.
  • Poor tactics, ordinary negligence, or an immaterial report error.
  • A violation of department policy.
  • Agency discipline—or the absence of discipline.

Facts that may justify specialist review

  • Objective evidence showing the arrest lacked a reasonable basis for probable cause.
  • Deliberate or reckless material lies or omissions in a report or warrant application.
  • Fabricated evidence or a baseless charge that caused or prolonged detention.
  • An unlawful home entry, clearly unreasonable search, or gratuitous force.
  • Evidence of statutory bad faith, malicious purpose, or wanton and willful conduct.
  • Documented harm directly caused by the challenged conduct.

The Bottom Line

A dismissal may expose facts worth investigating, but it does not create a lawsuit by itself. The stronger cases generally involve more than a mistake: a clearly unlawful arrest, search, entry, or use of force; materially false or fabricated evidence; provable causation and harm; and a legally responsible defendant who is not protected from the requested remedy.

The first step is preserving the dismissal order, suppression ruling, police reports, body-camera video, warrant materials, and proof of loss. A civil-rights lawyer should be consulted promptly because civil notice and filing deadlines can run independently of the criminal case.
