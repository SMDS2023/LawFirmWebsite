Can a Flock Camera Give You a Ticket in Florida? ALPR Law

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | August 15, 2026
Traffic Stops License Plate Readers Your Rights

Flock cameras are suddenly everywhere in the news. WUSF reported in early August that both major candidates for governor want limits on them — one calling for a full pause on Flock contracts, the other for warrant requirements. News 6 reported cameras vandalized in Volusia and Lake counties, and WFTV covered a Central Florida protester who went viral blocking the lenses with signs. With all that noise, drivers keep asking me the same question: can one of these cameras mail me a ticket? The short answer is no — but what they actually do matters more for your rights than most people realize.

What a Flock Camera Actually Records

An automated license plate reader, or ALPR, is a fixed camera that photographs every plate that passes it. The system captures your plate number, your vehicle's make, model, color, and distinguishing details, plus a timestamp and the camera's location. That's it. It does not measure your speed, it does not photograph the driver, and it does not generate a citation. When I patrolled for the Florida Highway Patrol, a ticket required a trooper who observed a violation. A Flock camera observes nothing but a plate passing a point on the road — which is exactly why your rights during a Florida traffic stop still depend on a human officer, not a machine.

What Florida Law Says

F.S. 316.0777(2)(b) is explicit: an automated license plate recognition system "may not be used to issue a notice of violation for a traffic infraction or a uniform traffic citation." Under Florida law, no ALPR — Flock or otherwise — can ticket you on its own. Ever.

A Searchable Network, Not a Ticket Camera

So why the political fight? Because the real function of these cameras is the database behind them. Every capture feeds a searchable network that participating agencies can query — and as TechCrunch reported in its national coverage of the backlash, that cross-agency, shared-database design is what has people uneasy. F.S. 316.0777 authorizes law enforcement to install ALPRs in state rights-of-way and makes the images and personal identifying data confidential and exempt from public records, while its companion statute, F.S. 316.0778, requires a retention schedule capping how long the data can be kept. The camera isn't judging your driving. It's building a record of where your vehicle has been — a record an officer can act on later.

From Database Hit to Traffic Stop

Here's how a plate capture becomes flashing lights in your mirror in the Orlando and Orange County area. The camera reads your plate and the system checks it against hotlists — stolen vehicles and wanted persons in FCIC/NCIC, and DHSMV records like expired or suspended registrations. A match pushes a real-time alert to a deputy's in-car terminal or through dispatch. Then comes the step that matters most: a human officer decides whether to stop you.

That step isn't optional courtesy. FDLE's statewide guidance — the CJJIS Council's Guidelines for the Use of Automated License Plate Readers, revised in November 2024 — directs officers to treat an ALPR alert as investigative information only: visually confirm that the plate the camera read matches the plate actually on the vehicle, and verify through dispatch or FCIC that the hit is still active before initiating a stop.

The Expired Tag Trap

Expired registration is a common hotlist category, and it escalates. Under F.S. 320.07, driving on an expired registration is a noncriminal infraction — but a registration expired more than six months, on a second or subsequent offense, is a second-degree criminal misdemeanor under F.S. 320.07(3)(c). A camera hit on an old tag can still turn a routine drive into a criminal case for repeat offenses.

Where the Human Step Can Go Wrong

ALPRs read plates with optical character recognition, and OCR makes mistakes. Databases hold stale entries. Officers under pressure sometimes stop first and verify later. Each of those failure points is a place your defense can start.

Common ALPR Failure Points

  • Misread characters: a B read as an 8, an O as a 0 — the alert fires on a plate that isn't yours.
  • Wrong-state plates: the same number can exist in multiple states; the hotlist entry may belong to a different vehicle entirely.
  • Obscured or framed plates: F.S. 316.605 requires a securely fastened, plainly visible plate with legible characters — a covered plate invites both misreads and a stop of its own, as I explained in my post on Florida's license plate frame law.
  • Stale hits: a vehicle recovered weeks ago, a warrant already served, a registration renewed yesterday — the database doesn't always keep up.

Why Verification Matters for Your Defense

Florida courts have drawn real lines here. In State v. Van Teamer, 151 So. 3d 421 (Fla. 2014), the Florida Supreme Court held that a database return showing a color discrepancy between a vehicle and its registration, standing alone, did not create reasonable suspicion for a stop — a computer return by itself is not automatically enough. And when the record itself is wrong, the remedy is suppression: in Shadler v. State, 761 So. 2d 279 (Fla. 2000), evidence was suppressed where a stop rested on an erroneous DHSMV record wrongly showing a suspended license, and in State v. White, 660 So. 2d 664 (Fla. 1995), evidence was suppressed where an arrest rested on a stale record of a warrant that was no longer active. If a deputy stopped you on a bare, unverified alert — or on a hit that was wrong or outdated — the stop's legality is on the table, and everything found during it may be challengeable through a motion to suppress. That's the same principle behind how probable cause works in Florida criminal cases, and it's how cases get dismissed when reasonable suspicion is missing.

What to Do After a Camera-Flagged Stop

If you're pulled over and the deputy mentions a plate reader, an alert, or a "hit," stay calm and be polite — the roadside is not the place to argue the database. Note exactly what the officer said prompted the stop, when and where it happened, and what was checked before you were pulled over. Whether you left with a ticket, a notice to appear, or a criminal charge — and the difference between a citation and a notice to appear matters more than most drivers realize — talk to a defense attorney before you fight it alone. The details of how the alert was verified, or wasn't, are usually invisible until a lawyer digs into the records.

How a Criminal Defense Attorney Can Help

If a camera-flagged stop led to charges in Orlando, an experienced defense attorney can pull the ALPR alert data, the dispatch traffic, and the hotlist records to test whether the stop was lawful from the first second. As a former State Trooper and Deputy Sheriff, I know what verification is supposed to look like before a stop — and I know how to show a court when it didn't happen. If the hit was misread, stale, or never corroborated, the evidence that followed may never reach a jury.
