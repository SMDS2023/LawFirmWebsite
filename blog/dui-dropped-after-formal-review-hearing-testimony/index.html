Lose the Battle, Win the War: A Lost DMV Hearing Ended a DUI

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | October 15, 2026
Case Result DUI Defense License Suspension

We lost the license hearing. We won the DUI case.

The DMV upheld our client's suspension. But that hearing put the arresting officer under oath, and the officer's account didn't match the body camera video. That gap became a motion to suppress, and the State dropped the DUI charge.

Here is why a license hearing is worth fighting even when you expect to lose it.

What Does "Lose the Battle, Win the War" Mean in a DUI Case?

A DUI arrest starts two separate cases. The criminal charge under F.S. 316.193 goes through the court. The license suspension goes through the DMV.

The license hearing is the battle. It comes first, the rules favor the State, and you may lose it. The criminal case is the war. That is where a conviction and your record are on the line.

Why Fight a License Hearing You Might Lose?

Two reasons. First, you might win. The odds are small, but if you win, the DUI comes off your driving record.

Second, the hearing is where you take the officer's testimony. At your request, the hearing officer subpoenas the arresting officer under F.S. 322.2615(6)(b). The officer answers your attorney's questions under oath, and those answers are locked in.

Most of these hearings happen by phone. That relaxed setting can lead to a lack of preparation. In this case, the officer had not watched the body camera video before testifying.

You have 10 days from the arrest to request the hearing under F.S. 322.2615(1)(b)3. See how another formal review hearing turned on one box on the citation.

Should You Waive the Hearing Instead?

You may have another option. On a first DUI, you can waive the formal review hearing under F.S. 322.271(7). That is true whether you blew over the limit or refused the test. You have to do DUI school first. Then you get a hardship license for the rest of the suspension.

The trade-off is real. Waiving gives up the hearing, and with it the chance to put the officer under oath.

If you request the hearing and lose, a first suspension runs 6 months if you blew 0.08 or higher. It runs 12 months if you refused the test. You start with a hard suspension: 30 days after a breath result over the limit, or 90 days after a refusal. After that, you can get a hardship license for the rest of the suspension.

The right choice depends on how much you need to drive and what the stop looks like. In this case, the hearing was worth it. Talk it through with your attorney before the deadline runs.

How Did the Testimony Conflict With the Video?

Our client was stepping out of a parked car. The officer was already nearby on an unrelated call. The officer stopped her, asked for ID, and asked her to do roadside exercises.

At the hearing, I asked whether she was free to go at that moment. The officer said no. The officer agreed that this was, in effect, a traffic stop.

Testifying from memory, the officer also described seeing signs that the car was in use and that our client was unsteady on her feet. Then we compared that sworn account with the body camera. The video showed no taillights, no brake lights, and nothing else to suggest the car was running. It also showed the officer standing several car lengths away.

Why the Contradiction Mattered

An officer can't hold you without reasonable suspicion of a crime. Once the officer admitted our client was not free to go, the stop had to rest on what the officer could see at that moment. The video showed less than the officer described. Whether a person in a parked car is "driving" is its own question, covered in our post on actual physical control.

Did We Win the License Hearing?

No. The hearing officer upheld the suspension. The DMV only needs a preponderance of the evidence.

That was the battle. But the officer's testimony was now locked in under oath, and it didn't match the video.

How Did a Lost Hearing Win the Criminal Case?

Three days after the license hearing, we filed a motion to suppress. It argued the detention began without the reasonable suspicion that F.S. 901.151 requires. If the stop was unlawful, the evidence that followed comes out: the roadside exercises, the statements, and the breath test.

In criminal court, the officer would face the same video and the same sworn answers. The State would have to defend the stop on that record.

We also filed a separate motion attacking the breath test. The instrument's O-rings had been replaced at its annual inspection. Read more about the O-ring issue.

The State dropped the case six days before the suppression hearing. Its written reason:

"Actions by law enforcement would result in suppression of evidence."

Does This Happen in Every DUI Case?

No. Most license hearings don't produce testimony like this. Many stops are lawful, and the video backs up the officer.

But you won't know until someone puts the officer under oath and checks the answers against the video. Each case has to be evaluated on its own facts.

What Should You Do After a DUI Arrest?

  • Decide: hearing or waiver: Ask for formal review within 10 days of the arrest, or weigh the waiver for a work license with your attorney.
  • Subpoena the officer: Get sworn answers before the officer has prepared.
  • Get the video: Body camera and dash camera footage show what the officer actually saw.
  • Use it in court: Build the criminal defense on every place the testimony and the video disagree.

Past results do not guarantee a similar outcome. Every case depends on its own facts. This article is general information about Florida law, not legal advice for your situation.
