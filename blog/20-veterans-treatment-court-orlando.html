What is Veterans Treatment Court?

Veterans Treatment Court is a specialized program operated by the Ninth Judicial Circuit Court of Florida that offers an alternative path for veterans facing certain criminal charges. Rather than traditional prosecution that could lead to conviction and incarceration, Veterans Treatment Court focuses on rehabilitation and treatment.

The program recognizes a fundamental truth: many veterans struggle with service-related issues that can lead to criminal behavior. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), traumatic brain injury (TBI), substance abuse, depression, and difficulty readjusting to civilian life are common among combat veterans—and these issues can sometimes manifest in ways that bring veterans into contact with the criminal justice system.

Core Philosophy of Veterans Court

  • Rehabilitation over Punishment: Focus on treating underlying issues rather than simply punishing criminal behavior
  • Recognition of Service: Acknowledgment that your service to the country deserves special consideration
  • Holistic Treatment: Addressing mental health, substance abuse, and readjustment challenges comprehensively
  • Peer Support: Connection with fellow veterans who understand the unique challenges you face
  • Second Chance: Opportunity to avoid a criminal conviction that could impact your future employment, housing, and benefits

Who Qualifies for Veterans Treatment Court?

According to the Ninth Judicial Circuit's Veterans Treatment Court program, eligibility generally requires:

✓

Veteran Status

You must have served in the United States Armed Forces. This includes active duty service in the Army, Navy, Air Force, Marines, Coast Guard, or service in the National Guard or Reserves with qualifying active duty time.

✓

Charged with Qualifying Crimes

The program accepts veterans charged with "certain crimes." While the specific crimes aren't detailed publicly, Veterans Treatment Courts typically accept cases involving non-violent offenses, certain misdemeanors, and some felony charges. Violent crimes, sexual offenses, and serious felonies are generally excluded.

✓

Service-Related Issues

Veterans suffering from combat-related trauma, PTSD, traumatic brain injury (TBI), substance abuse issues, or difficulties readjusting to civilian life are prioritized. The program aims to address these underlying issues that may have contributed to the criminal behavior.

✓

Meet Program Requirements

You must be willing and able to participate in ongoing assessments, counseling, treatment, and frequent court hearings. This requires a genuine commitment to rehabilitation and recovery.

⚠️ Important Note:

Eligibility is determined on a case-by-case basis. Even if you think you might not qualify, it's worth having an experienced attorney review your case and advocate for your acceptance into the program.

How Does Veterans Treatment Court Work?

The Veterans Treatment Court program is structured to provide comprehensive support while maintaining accountability. Here's what participants can expect:

1. Referral and Application

Interested veterans must submit a referral form directly to the Ninth Judicial Circuit Court Problem Solving Court office. This is typically done with the assistance of a criminal defense attorney who can ensure your application presents your case in the best possible light and includes all necessary documentation of your military service and service-related issues.

2. Assessment Phase

Once referred, you'll undergo a comprehensive assessment that includes:

  • Review of your military service record
  • Mental health evaluation
  • Substance abuse screening (if applicable)
  • Risk assessment
  • Evaluation of support systems and housing stability
  • Connection with VA resources

3. Treatment Plan Development

If approved for the program, a customized treatment plan will be developed specifically for your needs. This may include:

  • Mental Health Counseling: Individual and/or group therapy for PTSD, depression, anxiety, or other mental health issues
  • Substance Abuse Treatment: If substance abuse is a factor, treatment programs ranging from outpatient counseling to intensive inpatient rehabilitation
  • Medication Management: Working with medical professionals to properly manage medications for mental health conditions
  • VA Services: Connection to Department of Veterans Affairs resources and benefits you may be entitled to
  • Vocational Training: Job skills training and employment assistance
  • Housing Support: Assistance with stable housing arrangements
  • Peer Mentorship: Connection with a veteran mentor who has successfully completed the program

4. Active Participation and Court Monitoring

Veterans Treatment Court requires frequent court hearings where you'll appear before the judge to report on your progress. These hearings typically occur:

  • Weekly or bi-weekly in the early phases
  • Less frequently as you progress through the program
  • Before a judge who understands military culture and veteran issues

You'll also be subject to:

  • Ongoing assessments to monitor your progress and adjust treatment as needed
  • Random drug and alcohol testing (if substance abuse is part of your case)
  • Compliance checks to ensure you're attending counseling and treatment sessions
  • Regular contact with case managers and treatment providers

5. Phases and Milestones

Most Veterans Treatment Court programs operate in phases, with each phase requiring:

  • Completion of specific treatment goals
  • Clean drug tests (if applicable)
  • Regular attendance at all required sessions and court hearings
  • Demonstration of behavioral changes and progress
  • Increased responsibility and independence as you advance

6. Graduation and Case Resolution

Upon successful completion of all program requirements, participants typically receive:

  • Dismissal of charges or significant charge reduction
  • Avoidance of a criminal conviction on your record
  • Potential eligibility for record sealing or expungement
  • Connection to ongoing VA support and resources
  • Graduation ceremony recognizing your achievement

Program Duration

While the Ninth Judicial Circuit doesn't specify an exact timeline publicly, most Veterans Treatment Court programs nationwide last between 12 to 24 months, depending on individual progress and the severity of the underlying issues. Your commitment and engagement directly impact how quickly you can complete the program.

Common Misconceptions About Veterans Court

Myth #1: "It's an Easy Way Out"

Reality: Veterans Treatment Court is demanding and requires significant commitment. Participants must attend frequent court hearings, complete intensive treatment programs, maintain sobriety (if applicable), and make substantial lifestyle changes. Many participants find it more challenging than traditional sentencing—but far more rewarding.

Myth #2: "Any Veteran with Any Charge Qualifies"

Reality: Eligibility is limited to certain types of offenses, and acceptance into the program is not automatic. Violent crimes, sexual offenses, and serious felonies are typically excluded. Each case is evaluated individually.

Myth #3: "I Don't Need a Lawyer"

Reality: Having experienced legal representation is crucial. An attorney can:

  • Advocate for your acceptance into the program
  • Gather documentation of your military service and service-related issues
  • Negotiate with prosecutors to approve your participation
  • Ensure your rights are protected throughout the process
  • Provide guidance if you're rejected from the program or struggle during participation
  • Help navigate complex VA benefits issues that may arise

Myth #4: "Only Combat Veterans Qualify"

Reality: While combat-related trauma is common among participants, veterans who served in non-combat roles may still qualify if they developed service-related mental health or substance abuse issues during their military service.

The Application Process: How to Get Started

If you're a veteran facing criminal charges in Orange County and think Veterans Treatment Court might be right for you, here's how to proceed:

Step 1: Consult with an Attorney Immediately

Time is often critical. The earlier you begin exploring Veterans Court as an option, the better. Contact a criminal defense attorney experienced with Veterans Treatment Court to discuss your case.

Step 2: Gather Your Military Documentation

You'll need to provide proof of your military service and any service-related issues. This may include:

  • DD-214 (Certificate of Release or Discharge from Active Duty)
  • Military service records
  • VA disability rating documents
  • Medical records showing PTSD, TBI, or other diagnoses
  • Deployment records
  • Any documentation of combat service or exposure to traumatic events

Step 3: Complete the Referral Form

Your attorney will help you complete and submit the referral form to the Ninth Judicial Circuit Court Problem Solving Court office. This form should present your case in the strongest possible light while being completely honest about your situation.

Step 4: Undergo Assessment

If your referral is accepted for consideration, you'll undergo comprehensive mental health and substance abuse assessments to determine your needs and suitability for the program.

Step 5: Await Decision

The court, in consultation with prosecutors and treatment professionals, will decide whether to accept you into Veterans Treatment Court. Your attorney can advocate for your acceptance during this process.

Step 6: Begin Treatment

If accepted, you'll immediately begin your treatment plan and regular court appearances.

What If I'm Not Accepted?

If you're not accepted into Veterans Treatment Court, all is not lost. An experienced criminal defense attorney can still:

  • Negotiate for reduced charges or alternative sentencing
  • Present evidence of your military service and service-related issues to the judge for consideration at sentencing
  • Pursue other diversion programs that may be available
  • Build a strong defense to fight the charges at trial
  • Advocate for probation rather than incarceration
  • Help you access VA mental health and substance abuse treatment independently

National Context: Veterans Courts Across America

Orange County's Veterans Treatment Court is part of a nationwide movement. Since the first Veterans Treatment Court was established in Buffalo, New York in 2008, the concept has spread to hundreds of jurisdictions across the United States. These programs share common goals:

  • Reducing recidivism among veterans through proper diagnosis and treatment
  • Addressing the root causes of criminal behavior, not just the symptoms
  • Connecting veterans with earned VA benefits and services
  • Recognizing the unique needs of military veterans and service-related trauma
  • Providing accountability while offering compassion and understanding
  • Utilizing veteran mentors who play supportive roles throughout the process

The Power of Veteran Mentorship

One of the most impactful elements of Veterans Treatment Court is the mentor program. Veteran mentors—who have often successfully completed the program themselves or have experience helping fellow veterans—play a crucial supportive role. These mentors:

  • Provide peer support from someone who truly understands military service and veteran struggles
  • Offer guidance and encouragement throughout the treatment process
  • Help participants navigate challenges and stay committed to recovery
  • Serve as living proof that successful completion is possible
  • Bridge the gap between the court, treatment providers, and the veteran participant
  • Reduce feelings of alienation and isolation that many veterans experience

The mentor relationship addresses one of the core issues many veterans face: alienation and lack of support after returning from combat. Veterans who have difficulty reintegrating into civilian society often lack the camaraderie and support structure they had in the military. Mentors help fill this gap, providing the veteran-to-veteran connection that can make all the difference in recovery.

Related Issues for Veterans in the Criminal Justice System

PTSD and Criminal Defense

Post-traumatic stress disorder can manifest in behaviors that lead to criminal charges—hypervigilance, aggression, self-medication with alcohol or drugs, difficulty controlling emotions. Courts are increasingly recognizing PTSD as a mitigating factor, but proper documentation and expert testimony are essential. Learn more about how military service impacts criminal defense.

Substance Abuse and Veterans

Veterans face higher rates of substance abuse than the general population, often as a form of self-medication for PTSD, chronic pain, or other service-related issues. Drug offense charges can be addressed through Veterans Treatment Court's comprehensive treatment approach.

Domestic Violence and Military Culture

The stresses of military life—frequent deployments, family separation, combat trauma—can contribute to domestic conflicts. Domestic violence charges involving veterans require sensitive handling that addresses underlying trauma while ensuring victim safety.

VA Benefits and Criminal Charges

A criminal conviction can impact your ability to receive certain VA benefits. Veterans Treatment Court offers a path to resolve charges without conviction, protecting your earned benefits. Additionally, successfully documenting service-connected conditions for Veterans Court can support VA disability claims.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does Veterans Treatment Court take to complete?

Most programs last 12-24 months, though this varies based on individual progress and treatment needs. Your commitment to the program directly impacts completion time.

Will I have a criminal record after completing Veterans Court?

Successful completion typically results in charges being dismissed. Depending on the specific resolution, you may then be eligible to have the arrest record sealed or expunged, which would remove it from public view.

Can I work while in Veterans Treatment Court?

Yes, maintaining employment is encouraged and supported. The program is designed to allow you to work while participating in treatment and court appearances.

What if I'm already receiving VA disability for PTSD?

This actually strengthens your Veterans Court application, as it documents your service-connected condition. VA disability ratings are helpful evidence of service-related issues.

What happens if I fail drug tests or miss appointments?

The program uses a system of sanctions and incentives. Minor violations may result in increased supervision, additional treatment requirements, or brief jail stays. Serious or repeated violations can result in removal from the program, at which point you'd face traditional prosecution.

Do I need an honorable discharge?

While an honorable discharge is ideal, some programs accept veterans with general discharges under honorable conditions. Dishonorable discharges typically disqualify veterans from the program, though exceptions may exist. Consult with an attorney about your specific discharge status.

Will participating in Veterans Court affect my security clearance?

This depends on your specific situation and the nature of the charges. While completing Veterans Court is generally viewed more favorably than a criminal conviction, security clearance issues require careful analysis. Discuss this with your attorney and your security officer.
