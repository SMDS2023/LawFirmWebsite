Florida Splits Leave-Scene Into Two Statutes

Florida does not have one hit-and-run crime. Property damage is F.S. 316.061. Injury and death are F.S. 316.027. They share a duty to stop. They do not share a degree.

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | August 31, 2026
Leave Scene 316.061 316.027

A crash on a Sunday morning at Central Florida Parkway and International Drive is why I am writing this. ClickOrlando reported on August 30, 2026, that the Florida Highway Patrol charged a driver with leaving the scene of a crash involving death, and with DUI manslaughter, after that wreck. One person died. Another person was seriously injured. FHP said the driver left on foot. Passengers stayed.

I am not going to name anyone. I did not try the case. I practice criminal defense in Orlando. The reporting is the peg. The statutes are the point.

Florida does not have one “hit and run” crime. Property damage is F.S. 316.061. Injury and death are F.S. 316.027. They share a duty to stop. They do not share a degree.

Property damage is 316.061

This is the 2025 Florida Statutes text, still current:

The driver of any vehicle involved in a crash resulting only in damage to a vehicle or other property which is driven or attended by any person shall immediately stop such vehicle at the scene of such crash or as close thereto as possible, and shall forthwith return to, and in every event shall remain at, the scene of the crash until he or she has fulfilled the requirements of s. 316.062.

A person who violates that subsection commits a misdemeanor of the second degree. The court may order restitution for damage the driver’s vehicle caused.

The word that does the work is “only.” 316.061 is the property-damage statute. It is written for a crash that results only in damage to an attended vehicle or other attended property.

I have already written about a 316.061 case on private property and about a 316.061 charge that ended as a civil infraction. Those are case results. This post is the ladder.

Injury and death are 316.027

316.027 is titled “Crash involving death or personal injuries.” It applies to a crash on public or private property. It is not one felony. It is three.

If the crash results in injury to a person other than serious bodily injury, the driver must stop and remain until 316.062 is fulfilled. A person who willfully violates that paragraph commits a felony of the third degree.

If the crash results in serious bodily injury, it is a felony of the second degree. 316.027(1)(a) defines serious bodily injury as an injury, including to the driver, that creates a substantial risk of death, serious personal disfigurement, or protracted loss or impairment of the function of a bodily member or organ.

If the crash results in the death of a person, it is a felony of the first degree. The statute adds a mandatory minimum term of imprisonment of 4 years. A person who willfully commits that death offense while driving under the influence as set forth in 316.193(1) is also sentenced to a mandatory minimum of 4 years. A defendant may move to depart from that death minimum unless the violation was committed while driving under the influence. The court may grant the motion only if it finds that imposing the minimum would constitute or result in an injustice, and it has to say so in open court.

A conviction under any of those three 316.027 paragraphs also revokes the driver license for at least 3 years, as the statute points to 322.28(4).

The ClickOrlando story is a death case on FHP’s charging description. That is the 316.027(2)(c) rung, not 316.061. I am not going to invent a points schedule, a prison-year chart from chapter 775, or a result in that file.

What you have to do once you stop

Both statutes send you to F.S. 316.062. That section is the duty to give information and render aid.

The driver of a vehicle involved in a crash that injures or kills a person, or that damages an attended vehicle or other attended property, shall give name, address, and the registration number of the vehicle. Upon request, and if available, the driver shall exhibit a license or permit to drive, to an injured person or to the driver, occupant, or person attending damaged property, and to a police officer at the scene or investigating. The driver shall also render reasonable assistance to an injured person, including arranging to get that person to a physician, surgeon, or hospital if it is apparent that treatment is necessary, or if the injured person asks.

Stopping in the travel lane and doing nothing else is not the whole duty. Leaving before 316.062 is done is the problem both leave-scene statutes describe.

316.062(3) is a separate sentence I am not going to bury: the statutory duty to make a report or give information to a law enforcement officer making a written crash report does not extend to information that would violate the privilege against self-incrimination. That is not a rewrite of my corpus delicti post. It is the statute sitting next to the stop-and-remain duty.

Remaining is not the same as blocking the road

Both 316.061 and 316.027 say the stop must be made without unnecessarily obstructing traffic. If a damaged vehicle is blocking the regular flow, the driver must make every reasonable effort to move it, or have it moved. Failing that subsection is a nonmoving violation under chapter 318. It is not the leave-scene crime.

Moving a wreck out of a lane, when the statute tells you to, is not the same as leaving.

The ladder, without a brochure

  • Attended property damage only: 316.061. Second-degree misdemeanor.
  • Injury other than serious bodily injury: 316.027(2)(a). Third-degree felony, willfully.
  • Serious bodily injury: 316.027(2)(b). Second-degree felony, willfully.
  • Death: 316.027(2)(c). First-degree felony, willfully, with the 4-year minimum the statute writes.

The news story this week is the death rung plus a separate DUI manslaughter charge FHP announced. Two files can start from one crash. Leave-scene is one of them. DUI is the other. I wrote the DUI map elsewhere. This is the leave-scene map.
