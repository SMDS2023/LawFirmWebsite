Miss a Bond Check-In? That Could Be a New Criminal Charge Under Florida's HB 397

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | March 20, 2026
Criminal Defense Legal Guide

If you're out on bond in Florida right now, a bill moving through the legislature could change the stakes of your pretrial release conditions dramatically. HB 397 would make violating certain conditions of pretrial release a separate criminal offense -- meaning a missed check-in or a curfew slip wouldn't just risk your bond. It could result in a brand-new criminal charge.

Here's what you need to understand about this proposed legislation, how it would change current law, and what you should be doing right now if you're on pretrial release.

How Pretrial Release Violations Work Under Current Law

Right now, if you violate a condition of your pretrial release -- you miss a check-in with pretrial services, you break curfew, you fail a drug test, you forget to charge your GPS ankle monitor -- the consequences are serious, but they stay within your existing case.

A judge can revoke your bond, issue a warrant for your arrest, and hold you in jail until your case resolves. You might lose your ability to fight your case from the outside. That's a significant consequence, and it's one that defense attorneys deal with regularly.

But here's the critical distinction: under current Florida law, a pretrial release violation is not a separate criminal offense. You don't get a new charge. You don't get a new case number. The violation is handled as a bond issue within the case you already have pending.

Current Law Summary

  • Violation consequence: Bond revocation, re-arrest, potential jail hold
  • Legal effect: Handled within the existing case
  • No new charge: The violation itself is not a separate criminal offense
  • Judge's discretion: The court decides whether to revoke bond or modify conditions

What HB 397 Would Change

HB 397, currently pending in the Florida Legislature, would fundamentally alter this framework. The bill proposes making a violation of pretrial release conditions on certain violent charges a separate criminal offense. That means a new charge, a new case, and new penalties -- stacked on top of whatever you're already facing.

The bill specifically targets defendants charged with violent offenses. If you're on pretrial release for a qualifying violent crime and you violate any condition of your release, that violation itself would become an independently prosecutable offense.

Key Change Under HB 397

If passed, a pretrial release violation would no longer be just a bond issue. It would be a new criminal charge filed separately from your original case. You would face prosecution on both the original offense and the violation.

Which Charges Would Be Affected

HB 397 would not apply to every criminal case. The bill targets defendants on pretrial release for violent offenses -- categories that include charges such as aggravated assault, aggravated battery, robbery, domestic violence offenses, sexual battery, and other crimes involving violence or the threat of violence.

If your pending charge falls within the bill's covered categories and you violate any condition of your release, you would face the new separate charge. The exact scope of "violent offenses" under the bill is defined by the specific statutory categories referenced in the legislation.

Warrantless Arrest Authority

One of the most significant provisions in HB 397 is what it would do to arrest procedures. Under the proposed bill, law enforcement officers would be authorized to arrest someone for a pretrial release violation without a warrant.

Currently, when a pretrial release violation is detected, the typical process involves pretrial services notifying the court, a judge reviewing the violation, and the court issuing a warrant or a notice for the defendant to appear. There is a judicial check in the process.

HB 397 would allow officers to bypass that step for qualifying violations. If an officer has probable cause to believe you violated a condition of your pretrial release on a violent charge, they could arrest you on the spot -- no warrant, no judicial review before the arrest.

The Cascading Problem: One Violation, Two Cases

This is where the practical impact of HB 397 would hit hardest. Consider this scenario:

How the Cascade Would Work

  1. Original charge: You're out on bond for a violent offense. You're complying with GPS monitoring, curfew, and weekly check-ins.
  2. The violation: Your GPS monitor battery dies overnight, or you're 30 minutes late to a check-in, or you test positive for a substance.
  3. New charge filed: Under HB 397, that violation would become a separate criminal case -- a new charge on top of your original case.
  4. New bond required: You would now need bond on the new violation charge. But you just demonstrated that you couldn't comply with bond conditions -- making it harder to get bond.
  5. Held on both cases: A judge could deny bond on the new charge and revoke bond on the original charge. You're now sitting in jail on two open cases instead of one.

The cascading effect is the real danger. A single missed check-in could, if this bill passes, result in someone going from out on bond and working with their attorney to locked up on two cases with no realistic path to pretrial release.

The Defense Concern: Technical Violations vs. Willful Defiance

One of the most troubling aspects of this proposed legislation is what it doesn't address: the difference between a willful violation and a technical one.

Pretrial release conditions are detailed and demanding. GPS monitors need to be charged. Check-in offices have limited hours. Drug tests require appearing at specific locations during specific windows. Life doesn't always cooperate with those requirements, and not every failure to comply is an intentional act of defiance.

Scenarios That Could Trigger a New Charge

  • GPS monitor malfunction: The device loses power or signal through no fault of the wearer
  • Medical emergency: A hospital visit causes a missed check-in or curfew violation
  • Office closure: The pretrial services office is closed due to weather or staffing -- but the check-in was still "missed"
  • Transportation failure: No ride to a check-in, especially for defendants without a driver's license
  • Employer conflict: Work schedule prevents timely compliance with a condition

Under the current system, a judge evaluates the circumstances of a violation before deciding what to do. A reasonable explanation for a missed check-in might result in a warning or modified conditions rather than bond revocation. That judicial discretion serves as a safety valve.

If HB 397 passes, a technical violation could result in a new criminal charge before a judge even has the opportunity to assess whether the violation was intentional.

Due Process Questions

Converting administrative bond conditions into criminal offenses raises serious constitutional questions. Pretrial release conditions are imposed before any conviction -- they're conditions placed on someone who is presumed innocent. Making the violation of those conditions a separate crime effectively creates criminal liability for failing to comply with restrictions imposed on someone who hasn't been found guilty of anything yet.

Defense attorneys and civil liberties organizations are likely to challenge these provisions on due process grounds if the bill becomes law. The intersection of pretrial detention, the presumption of innocence, and the creation of new criminal liability from administrative conditions is legally contested territory.

What You Should Do If You're on Pretrial Release Right Now

Whether or not HB 397 passes, the practical advice is the same -- but the urgency is higher if this bill becomes law. If you're currently out on bond, especially on a violent charge, strict compliance with every condition of your release is critical.

Protect Yourself Now

  • Comply with every condition, every time. No exceptions. If your curfew is 10 PM, be home by 9:30 PM. If your check-in is Tuesday, don't wait until Wednesday.
  • Document everything. Keep a log of every check-in. Save receipts, screenshots, or confirmations showing you appeared when required. If your GPS monitor shows a charge level, note it daily.
  • Charge your GPS monitor religiously. Set alarms. Treat it like medication -- missing a charge is not an option.
  • Contact your attorney BEFORE you miss a requirement. If you know you can't make a check-in, if your monitor is malfunctioning, if you have a medical issue -- call your lawyer first. Proactive communication is your best protection.
  • Keep proof of compliance. If there's ever a dispute about whether you complied, you want documentation on your side. Photos of your charged monitor, timestamps of your arrivals, records of calls to pretrial services.
  • Don't assume small violations don't matter. Under current law, a judge might overlook a minor slip. If HB 397 passes, that same minor slip could be a new criminal charge.

Bottom Line

HB 397 is still a proposed bill -- it has not become law. But its trajectory through the Florida Legislature signals a shift toward treating pretrial release violations as independent criminal acts rather than bond management issues. If it passes, the consequences of a missed check-in, a curfew violation, or a GPS monitor issue would escalate from a bond hearing to a new arrest and a new prosecution.

If you're currently on pretrial release for a violent charge in Florida, the time to tighten your compliance is now. And if you have any concern about your ability to meet a condition of your release -- whether it's a scheduling conflict, a monitor issue, or a question about what's required -- contact your defense attorney immediately. Don't wait for the problem to become a charge.
