DUI While Asleep? Florida's Actual Physical Control Rule

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | August 17, 2026
DUI Defense Actual Physical Control Florida Law

Around 6:30 a.m. on August 13, an Ocoee police officer walked up to a car stopped at an intersection and found the driver sound asleep behind the wheel. According to ClickOrlando's reporting, bodycam footage shows she had been asleep for more than 20 minutes before the officer woke her — and she was arrested on suspicion of DUI even though no officer ever saw the car move.

The Ocoee Wake-Up Call

That incident — no names, no injuries, and police saying it could have been much worse — started with a caller reporting a driver asleep at an intersection. The officer woke her and began a DUI investigation on the spot. If that arrest seems intuitive because the car sat in a travel lane, here is the part that surprises people: Florida law does not require the car to be in a lane, running, or moving at all. The same statute reaches the person who deliberately parked, shut off the engine, and reclined the seat to "sleep it off." How that works starts with the statute itself — the same foundation covered in our complete Florida DUI defense guide.

What Florida Law Actually Says

F.S. 316.193 makes it a crime to be "driving or in actual physical control of a vehicle" while impaired or with a breath- or blood-alcohol level of .08 or above. That second clause is the entire ballgame in a sleeping-driver case. The Legislature deliberately wrote the statute to cover more than driving, and the impairment presumptions in F.S. 316.1934 expressly apply to actual physical control as well. Nothing in the statute requires the engine to be running.

What "Actual Physical Control" Means

Florida courts read the phrase as being in the vehicle with the capability to operate it — whether or not you ever do. A person asleep in the driver's seat who could wake up, start the car, and drive away can be "in control" for DUI purposes.

The Actual Physical Control Test

Decades of Florida appellate decisions built the factor framework. In Griffin v. State (Fla. 2d DCA 1984), a defendant found in the driver's seat with the keys in the ignition was properly convicted — the jury could infer he was able to start the car and drive away at any time. Fieselman v. State (Fla. 3d DCA 1988) held that even a cold, engine-off car did not defeat the charge when the keys sat in the ignition; that was a question for the jury. And Baltrus v. State (Fla. 4th DCA 1990) went further: keys merely in the sleeping occupant's hand were enough. From those cases, courts weigh three things:

  • Where you are in the vehicle: the driver's seat weighs heavily toward control; the back seat weighs against it.
  • Where the keys are: ignition, hand, or console within reach all support the charge; a key genuinely out of reach undercuts it.
  • Whether the car could actually run: under Jones v. State (Fla. 1st DCA 1987), a genuinely inoperable vehicle — there, one with a dead electrical system that had to be pushed to a repair shop — can defeat actual physical control.

As a former Florida Highway Patrol trooper, I can tell you officers are trained to document exactly these details. An actual-physical-control arrest almost never starts as a traffic stop — it starts as a welfare check or a 911 call about a stopped car, just like the Ocoee incident. Because nobody saw driving, the report leans hard on seat position, key location, and whether the engine or accessories were on, since the officer knows the driving element will be attacked later.

Sleeping It Off: The Good-Intentions Trap

Here is the frustrating irony: the person who realizes they shouldn't drive and pulls over to sleep is often doing exactly what safety campaigns urge — yet the driver's seat plus reachable keys can equal a DUI arrest anyway. Once that arrest happens, the machinery is identical to any other DUI. Implied consent under F.S. 316.1932 applies, officers may request field sobriety exercises and breath testing, and the post-arrest clock starts running.

The Stakes Went Up in 2025

Under Trenton's Law, effective October 1, 2025, refusing a lawful breath, urine, or blood test is now a criminal offense under F.S. 316.1939 — even a first refusal. And under F.S. 322.2615, a .08+ result or a refusal triggers an immediate administrative license suspension with a short deadline to request a formal review hearing. Both apply to actual-physical-control arrests just as they do to observed driving.

How These Cases Get Challenged

Because no one saw the car move, actual-physical-control cases have built-in weak points that ordinary DUI cases don't. The State must prove impairment at the time of control — but if you were asleep for an hour, when exactly were you impaired while in control? Courts have also recognized inoperability as a defense, and the factor framework cuts both ways: keys locked in the trunk or held by a friend tell a very different story than keys in the ignition. The legality of the initial encounter matters too — officers still need a lawful basis to detain and arrest, the same probable cause principles that govern any criminal case.

Common Defense Angles in APC Cases

  • The vehicle was inoperable and could not have been driven.
  • The keys were genuinely out of reach, not merely out of the ignition.
  • The State cannot prove when impairment occurred relative to control.
  • The detention or arrest lacked a lawful basis — one of several ways Florida DUI cases get dismissed.

Know Before You Rest

Florida is actively pushing impaired-driving enforcement — FLHSMV and FHP announced a statewide "Never Drive Impaired" campaign in March 2026 alongside national "Drive Sober or Get Pulled Over" mobilizations — so welfare checks on occupied parked cars aren't going away. The general takeaway I used to give at citizens' academies still holds: a person asleep in the back seat with the keys out of reach presents a far weaker case than one behind the wheel with keys in the ignition. And because these charges turn entirely on small factual details, any DUI built on actual physical control deserves a careful review of exactly what the officer saw and wrote down.
