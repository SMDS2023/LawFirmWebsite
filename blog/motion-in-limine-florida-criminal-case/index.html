Motion in Limine in Florida Criminal Cases, Explained

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | April 10, 2026
Criminal Defense Pre-Trial Motions Evidence

Some of the most important moments in a Florida criminal trial happen before a single juror walks into the courtroom. A motion in limine is a pre-trial request asking the judge to rule certain evidence or arguments off-limits — so the jury never hears them in the first place. If a motion to suppress polices what happened on the roadside, the motion in limine polices what happens in the courtroom.

What Is a Motion in Limine?

"In limine" is Latin for "at the threshold," and that is exactly where the motion lives. Before trial begins — often in the days or weeks leading up to jury selection — either side can ask the judge to decide in advance whether a specific piece of evidence, a line of testimony, or even a particular word or phrase will be allowed in front of the jury.

The logic is simple: some evidence is so damaging that no instruction to "disregard" it can truly undo the harm once jurors have heard it. You cannot unring a bell. A motion in limine gets the fight over with while the jury box is still empty, so the trial the jury actually sees has already been shaped by the judge's evidentiary rulings.

Motion in Limine vs. Motion to Suppress

These two motions are often confused, but they attack evidence from completely different angles. A motion to suppress argues that evidence was gathered illegally — an unlawful stop, arrest, or search that violated your constitutional rights, litigated under Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.190. If the judge agrees, the evidence is excluded because of how the government obtained it. That analysis often turns on questions like whether the officer had probable cause in the first place.

A motion in limine asks a different question entirely: even if the evidence was gathered lawfully, is it admissible under the Florida Evidence Code, Chapter 90? The baseline rule, F.S. 90.402, says relevant evidence is admissible except as provided by law — and the motion in limine argues that one of those exceptions applies. The stop can be perfectly legal and the evidence can still be kept from the jury because it is hearsay, unfairly prejudicial, or improper opinion.

The One-Sentence Version

A motion to suppress attacks how the evidence was gathered (constitutional grounds). A motion in limine attacks whether the jury should hear it (Florida Evidence Code grounds). Suppression polices the roadside; in limine polices the courtroom.

What Evidence Can Be Kept Out

In Florida criminal cases, motions in limine tend to target a few recurring categories under Chapter 90:

  • Prior bad acts and Williams Rule evidence: Under F.S. 90.404(2) — codifying the Florida Supreme Court's decision in Williams v. State, 110 So. 2d 654 (Fla. 1959) — evidence of other crimes or acts is admissible only to prove something specific like intent, plan, or identity, never just to paint the defendant as a bad person. In Woodard v. State, 978 So. 2d 217 (Fla. 1st DCA 2008), a conviction was reversed because a decades-old allegation proved nothing but propensity — a reminder of what happens when this evidence reaches a jury.
  • Unfairly prejudicial material: F.S. 90.403 lets the judge exclude even relevant evidence when its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice — the workhorse ground for gruesome photos and inflammatory language.
  • Hearsay: F.S. 90.802 bars out-of-court statements offered for their truth unless a statutory exception applies.
  • Improper opinion testimony: F.S. 90.701 limits lay witnesses to opinions based on their own perceptions that don't require specialized knowledge.

That last category made headlines in a high-profile Florida case: in the 2025 Tallahassee murder trial of Donna Adelson, Court TV reported that the defense filed motions in limine asking the judge to limit investigators' opinion testimony and to bar witnesses from "interpreting" emails, calls, and text messages for the jury. Whatever the charge, the principle is the same — witnesses testify to facts, and the jury draws the conclusions.

The DUI and Traffic Angle

As a former Florida Highway Patrol trooper, I can tell you that what an officer writes in a report and what an officer is legally allowed to say to a jury are two different things. In a DUI or criminal traffic case, in limine motions are used to keep the officer from offering the conclusory opinion that a driver "was drunk," to exclude test results where the State cannot lay the required predicate, and to bar references to uncharged conduct — even when the stop itself was completely lawful. It is the courtroom counterpart to discovery-violation fights like the one in our 103 MPH super speeder Richardson hearing.

How the Process Works in a Florida Courtroom

In Orange County and across Florida, motions in limine are typically filed in writing before trial and heard at a pre-trial hearing or on the morning of trial — always outside the jury's presence. Each side argues admissibility under Chapter 90, and the judge rules one of three ways:

The Three Possible Rulings

  • Granted: the evidence or argument is off-limits, and the other side cannot mention it in front of the jury — including during opening statements.
  • Denied: the evidence may come in, and the defense can plan its trial strategy knowing the jury will hear it.
  • Deferred: the judge reserves ruling until trial, waiting to see the context in which the evidence is actually offered.

Why Timing and Preservation Matter

Winning the motion is not the end of the story. Under F.S. 90.104, a definitive pre-trial ruling admitting or excluding evidence preserves the issue for appeal without a renewed objection at trial. But as The Florida Bar's guidance on preserving trial error makes clear, tentative or deferred rulings still require a contemporaneous objection or proffer when the moment arrives — and an objection on one ground does not preserve other grounds.

The Door Can Be Opened

Excluded evidence does not stay excluded unconditionally. If the defense "opens the door" — for example, by eliciting testimony that makes the barred material relevant — the judge can revisit the ruling mid-trial. And if the prosecution violates a granted motion in limine, defense counsel must still object on the spot to preserve the error. In limine practice demands discipline from the first question to the last.

The Bigger Picture: Shaping the Trial Before It Starts

A motion in limine rarely wins a case by itself. Its power is cumulative: combined with suppression motions, discovery enforcement through Richardson hearings, and careful trial preparation, it determines the version of events the jury is actually allowed to consider. By the time opening statements begin, a well-litigated case has already been narrowed — the inflammatory photo excluded, the "interpretation" testimony barred, the prior allegation kept out. That is pre-trial defense strategy working the way it should.
