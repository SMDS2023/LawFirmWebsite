Motion in Limine in Florida Criminal Cases, Explained

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | April 10, 2026
Criminal Defense Pre-Trial Motions Evidence

A motion in limine asks the judge to keep something away from the jury before trial starts. That something might be a prior arrest, a rumor, a photo, or one word a witness likes to use. In a Florida criminal case, the fight happens with the jury box empty. Win it, and the jury never knows the thing existed.

What Is a Motion in Limine?

Why fight about it early? Because once the jury hears it, no instruction can un-hear it. A judge can tell jurors to disregard a remark. Nobody believes that works every time.

So the motion goes in before trial, usually in writing. Either side can file one. The State files them too, often to keep you from raising something it does not want jurors to weigh.

The judge hears argument with no jurors in the room. That can happen at a pre-trial hearing weeks out, or on the morning of trial before opening statements.

Motion in Limine vs. Motion to Suppress

People confuse this motion with a motion to suppress. A motion to suppress attacks how police got the evidence. A motion in limine attacks whether the jury should hear it at all. You can lose the first and still win the second.

Suppression is a constitutional fight. Your lawyer argues the stop, the arrest, or the search was unlawful, so the evidence has to go. Whether the officer had probable cause usually decides it. We walk through that hearing in our post on what to expect at a motion to suppress hearing.

A motion in limine starts from a different rule. F.S. 90.402 says relevant evidence comes in unless some other law keeps it out. The motion points to that other law. The stop can be perfectly legal and the evidence can still never reach the jury.

The Short Version

Suppression polices the roadside. In limine polices the courtroom. One asks how the evidence was gathered. The other asks what the jury gets to hear about it.

What Evidence Can Be Kept Out?

Most motions in limine in Florida criminal cases aim at a short list of targets. The Florida Evidence Code, Chapter 90, supplies the grounds for each one.

Common Targets in a Florida Criminal Case

  • Prior bad acts. Florida calls this the Williams Rule, after Williams v. State, 110 So. 2d 654 (Fla. 1959). The State may use your other acts to prove intent, plan, or identity. It may not use them to suggest you are the kind of person who would do this. It must also give written notice first.
  • Unfairly prejudicial material. Gruesome photos, loaded labels, and anything that inflames more than it proves.
  • Hearsay. Out-of-court statements offered to prove they are true, unless an exception applies.
  • Improper opinion. A lay witness can describe what they saw. They cannot interpret it for the jury or offer conclusions that need expert training.
  • Inadmissible tests and uncharged conduct. Results without a proper foundation, and references to things you were never charged with.

The workhorse ground is F.S. 90.403. It lets the judge exclude relevant evidence when its value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion, or the risk of misleading the jury. That is the rule for the bloody photo and the inflammatory label.

Bad-acts evidence is where the stakes run highest. In Woodard v. State, 978 So. 2d 217 (Fla. 1st DCA 2008), a decades-old accusation reached the jury and proved nothing but bad character. The appeals court reversed the conviction and ordered a new trial without it.

Opinion testimony drew attention in a widely covered 2025 Tallahassee trial. Court TV reported that the defense asked the judge to limit investigators' opinions and bar witnesses from interpreting emails, calls, and texts. The principle is simple. Witnesses give facts. Jurors draw conclusions.

What This Looks Like in a DUI or Traffic Case

Say the stop was lawful and suppression fails. You still have in limine. Your lawyer can ask the judge to bar the officer from telling the jury you "were drunk." That is a conclusion, not an observation.

The same motion can keep out breath or eye-test results when the State cannot lay the required foundation. It can also block mention of uncharged conduct. It is the courtroom cousin of the discovery fight in our Richardson hearing that got the State's evidence excluded in a 103 mph case.

A breath-test refusal is different. Florida law generally lets the jury hear that you refused. A motion in limine reaches a refusal only in narrow cases, such as when the State cannot show you were properly warned of the consequences of refusing.

How the Process Works in a Florida Courtroom

The motion is filed in writing before trial. The judge hears it outside the jury's presence, often at a pre-trial hearing or on the morning of trial. Each side argues the evidence rule that applies. Then the judge rules one of three ways.

  • Granted: The evidence is off-limits. The other side cannot mention it, not even in opening statements.
  • Denied: The evidence comes in. You know that before jurors arrive, and the trial plan adjusts.
  • Deferred: The judge waits to see how the evidence comes up at trial. Your lawyer must raise it again at the right moment.

A denied motion still has value. You learn what the jury will hear, and your lawyer plans around it instead of being surprised mid-trial.

Why Timing and Preservation Matter

A win before trial is not always the end of it. F.S. 90.104 says a definitive ruling before or at trial preserves the issue for appeal without a fresh objection. A tentative or deferred ruling does not. Then your lawyer must object or proffer when the evidence comes up.

The Florida Bar's guidance on preserving trial error adds a wrinkle. An objection on one ground does not save the others. If the ground argued at trial differs from the one raised on appeal, the appellate court may never reach it.

The Door Can Be Opened

Excluded evidence can come back. If the defense asks a question that makes the barred material relevant, the judge can revisit the ruling mid-trial. And if the prosecutor violates a granted motion, your lawyer still has to object on the spot. Silence waives it.

How a Criminal Defense Attorney Can Help
