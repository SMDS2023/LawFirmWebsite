Hearsay in Florida Criminal Cases: F.S. 90.801

By Jeff Lotter, Criminal Defense Attorney | September 12, 2026
Criminal Defense Evidence Trial Practice

Florida's hearsay rule lets you keep what someone else said out of your trial. It works only if your defense makes the state prove an exception for every statement it wants the jury to hear. That fight usually happens before trial, in a motion in limine, and it decides what the jury ever learns.

What counts as hearsay?

F.S. 90.801 defines hearsay in three parts. There must be a statement, made by a declarant outside the courtroom, and offered to prove that what it says is true.

A statement can be spoken, written, or a gesture meant as an assertion, like a nod or pointing at someone. The declarant is simply the person who made it.

The third part is where most arguments live. If the state offers the words to prove they are true, that is hearsay. If it offers them for some other reason, such as why an officer walked to a certain door, it is not.

That is why "someone told me" is not always hearsay. The question is always what the words are being used to prove.

Three statements the statute treats as non-hearsay

The same statute treats three things as non-hearsay when the speaker testifies and faces cross-examination. They are prior inconsistent statements made under oath, prior consistent statements that answer a claim of recent fabrication, and an out-of-court identification. A witness who picked you at a show-up or photo array is the common example.

Who has to prove the statement comes in?

F.S. 90.802 makes hearsay inadmissible unless a statute says otherwise. The state carries the burden of fitting each statement into an exception, and the judge decides whether the foundation is there.

That burden runs statement by statement. A 911 call, a body-cam remark, and a line in the arrest affidavit each need their own exception. Layers inside a statement need one too, so a report repeating what a neighbor said has two hurdles.

The vehicle for that fight is a pretrial motion in limine. Win it and the jury never hears the words.

Which exceptions does the state use most?

Most hearsay disputes in Orange County criminal cases start with what patrol officers record. Body cameras capture the complaining witness's first account, dispatch keeps the 911 audio, and the affidavit summarizes both. When that witness skips trial, the state offers the recordings instead.

F.S. 90.803 lists the exceptions that apply whether or not the speaker shows up. In criminal court, four of them do most of the work.

  • Excited utterance: a statement made under the stress of a startling event, before there was time to think it over. The judge decides those facts first.
  • Spontaneous statement: a description of an event made while it is happening or right after. No startling event is needed, and answering a question does not defeat it.
  • Business and public records: records kept in the ordinary course, made at or near the time, with a custodian or certification. A document prepared for the case does not qualify.
  • Party admissions: your own words, to police or anyone else, come in against you. The state does not need a separate exception for what you said.

The "based on my investigation" problem

An officer may not tell the jury that he developed you as a suspect "based on the investigation" or after talking to witnesses. The Florida Supreme Court has held that testimony is backdoor hearsay. The only inference is that unnamed people implicated you. Object to it before it is said.

A recorded interview, written statement, or deposition-only account offered in place of live testimony

Does the Confrontation Clause add a second barrier?

Yes. The Sixth Amendment gives you the right to confront the witnesses against you. Under Crawford, a testimonial statement stays out unless the speaker is unavailable and you already had a real chance to cross-examine. That is true even when a hearsay exception fits.

The line is the primary purpose of the statement. A 911 caller describing an attack in progress is seeking help, so the statement is non-testimonial. A victim telling an officer who did it after the scene is secure is making an accusation for prosecution, and that is testimonial.

The Florida Supreme Court drew that line in State v. Lopez. An on-scene identification to a responding officer qualified as an excited utterance, but it was still testimonial. A discovery deposition did not count as prior cross-examination.

The same principle reaches lab work. In 2024 the U.S. Supreme Court held that a substitute analyst repeating an absent analyst's findings offers them for their truth, and testimonial findings violate the clause. That matters in DUI and drug cases, and it matters more as software-generated reports replace live witnesses.

A persuasive federal note

A February 2026 Eleventh Circuit decision, United States versus Holley, applied the federal rules, not Florida's. It let body-cam bystander statements in to explain the police response and admitted an anonymous 911 call as a present sense impression. Neither was testimonial. The reporter citation is unconfirmed, and the opinion is persuasive only in Florida court.

How do you keep hearsay out before trial?

File a motion in limine. It asks the judge to rule on each statement before jury selection, with time to argue foundation and confrontation. A hearsay motion is different from a motion to suppress, which attacks how police got the evidence rather than whether the jury should hear it.

F.S. 90.104 governs preservation. A definitive pretrial ruling does not need to be renewed at trial, but a tentative one does. If the judge reserves, your lawyer must object again, specifically and at the moment the statement is offered.

Florida courts have enforced that rule for decades. An objection that is late or vague usually waives the issue on appeal, because the trial judge never had a chance to fix it. Hearsay that comes in without objection is treated as if it were fine.

Losing the motion is not the end. Once a hearsay statement is admitted, the absent speaker's credibility can be attacked as if they had testified, including with their own inconsistent statements.
