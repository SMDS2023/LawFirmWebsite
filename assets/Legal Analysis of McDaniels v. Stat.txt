Legal Analysis of McDaniels v. State and the Unconstitutionality of Florida's Open Carry Ban
1.0 Introduction: A Landmark Shift in Florida's Firearm Laws
The decision by Florida's First District Court of Appeal in McDaniels v. State represents a pivotal moment in the state's Second Amendment jurisprudence. By declaring Florida’s long-standing ban on the open carrying of firearms unconstitutional, the ruling directly realigns state law with the U.S. Supreme Court's controlling "text, history, and tradition" framework. As the court itself noted, prior to this decision, Florida was an "outlier," standing with only a few other states that generally prohibited the open carrying of firearms. This ruling effectively invalidates a law enacted in 1987 and positions Florida with the majority of states that permit some form of open carry for law-abiding citizens.
The purpose of this document is to provide a neutral and objective analysis of the McDaniels decision. This analysis will deconstruct the court's reasoning, trace the evolution of the legal precedents that guided the ruling, and clarify the immediate impact of this decision on law enforcement and public conduct in Florida. To fully appreciate the significance of this holding, we will begin with the specific facts that brought this constitutional challenge before the court.
2.0 Factual and Procedural Background of the Case
Understanding the specific facts and procedural path of McDaniels v. State is essential for appreciating why Stanley McDaniels's actions created a definitive test case for Florida's open carry law in the post-Bruen era. His deliberate and peaceful conduct provided the court with a clear factual predicate, free of confounding factors, to evaluate the law's constitutionality.
The events leading to the legal challenge unfolded as a clear, chronological narrative:
• The Incident: On July 4, 2022, Stanley McDaniels stood at a major intersection in downtown Pensacola. In one hand, he held a copy of the United States Constitution, and with the other, he waved at passing vehicles. He had a loaded handgun in an inside-the-waistband holster, which was clearly visible. His conduct was peaceful, he was not threatening anyone, and he had set up a camera on a tripod to record his activity, signaling a planned constitutional challenge.
• Law Enforcement Interaction: When officers arrived several hours later, McDaniels was cooperative. He explained that his intent was to challenge the law and take the case to the supreme court. He provided officers with his Florida Concealed Carry permit.
• Arrest and Charges: Though officers at the scene did not arrest him, they later obtained a warrant for his arrest for violating section 790.053, Florida Statutes, also known as "Florida’s Open Carry Ban." McDaniels subsequently turned himself in.
Before his trial, McDaniels filed a motion to dismiss the charge, arguing that Florida's Open Carry Ban was unconstitutional under the Second Amendment. The trial court denied his motion but recognized the gravity of the legal issue. It certified a question of "great public importance" to the appellate court, framing the central constitutional conflict:
"DOES SECTION 790.053, FLORIDA STATUTES, VIOLATE THE SECOND AMENDMENT RIGHT TO BEAR ARMS IN PUBLIC CONSIDERING THE NATION’S HISTORICAL TRADITION OF FIREARM REGULATION?"
The appellate court was thus required to answer the certified question by applying the broad legal framework established by the U.S. Supreme Court.
3.0 The Controlling Jurisprudence: From Heller to Bruen
The McDaniels court did not operate in a vacuum. Its decision was the direct result of a dramatic, two-decade evolution in Second Amendment interpretation by the U.S. Supreme Court. The landmark cases that preceded McDaniels first created and then fundamentally altered the modern legal test for firearm regulations.
The Foundational Rulings: Heller and McDonald
The modern era of Second Amendment law began with District of Columbia v. Heller (2008). In that case, the Supreme Court established that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess firearms for self-defense, a right grounded in the amendment's text and historical understanding. Crucially, Heller rejected "freestanding 'interest-balancing' approach[es]" that would weigh an enumerated right against government policy objectives.
Two years later, in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010), the Court held that this individual right is "fundamental" and incorporated it against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. This decision prevented the Second Amendment from being treated as a "second-class right" and ensured its principles applied with equal force to state and local laws.
The Intermediate Era: The "Two-Step" Framework and Norman v. State
In the decade following Heller and McDonald, lower federal and state courts adopted a "two-step" means-end scrutiny framework to evaluate Second Amendment challenges. This approach first asked if a law burdened conduct protected by the Second Amendment and, if so, applied a level of judicial scrutiny—most often, "intermediate scrutiny"—to balance the law's burden against the government's public safety interests.
The Florida Supreme Court applied this exact framework in Norman v. State (2017). The court upheld Florida's Open Carry Ban by determining that while the law burdened the right to bear arms, it was a "reasonable fit" for the state's interest in public safety, especially since Florida offered a permissive concealed carry licensing regime as an alternative. The court deferred to the legislature's judgment, a hallmark of the means-end scrutiny approach. This decision represented the controlling state precedent on open carry before the legal landscape was upended.
The New Standard: New York State Rifle & Pistol Association, Inc. v. Bruen
The paradigm shifted completely with the Supreme Court's decision in New York State Rifle & Pistol Association, Inc. v. Bruen (2022). Bruen explicitly rejected the two-step, means-end framework used in Norman, calling it "one step too many." The Court restored the standard first articulated in Heller, mandating a strict "text, history, and tradition" test. The Court defined this standard as follows:
"When the Second Amendment’s plain text covers an individual’s conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct. The government must then justify its regulation by demonstrating that it is consistent with the Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation."
This new standard, binding on all lower courts, set the stage for the McDaniels court's direct application of these principles to Florida's law.
4.0 The Court's Application of the Bruen Framework
This section forms the analytical core of the McDaniels decision. The appellate court first had to establish why the new Bruen standard superseded existing state precedent before applying its rigorous historical test to Florida's Open Carry Ban.
Supremacy of Federal Law: Why Bruen Displaced Norman
The court's reasoning began with the U.S. Constitution's Supremacy Clause. It explained that because the U.S. Supreme Court is the final arbiter of federal constitutional law, its interpretation in Bruen is "the supreme Law of the Land." This principle makes the prior analysis in Norman, which was based on a framework that Bruen explicitly rejected, non-controlling. State courts are bound to follow the Supreme Court's current interpretation of the U.S. Constitution, regardless of their own prior rulings.
Applying the "Text, History, and Tradition" Test
With the Bruen framework established as the controlling standard, the court proceeded with its two-step analysis.
Step 1: The Plain Text Analysis
First, the court found that the Second Amendment's plain text, which protects the right to "bear arms," clearly covers the open carrying of firearms in public. It noted that the text makes no distinction between modes of carry. The State of Florida did not contest this point, effectively conceding that the conduct was presumptively protected by the Constitution.
Step 2: The Historical Tradition Analysis
Because the conduct was covered by the plain text, the burden shifted entirely to the State to prove that its ban on open carry was consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. The court methodically deconstructed the State's arguments and found them insufficient.
• Rejection of Analogues: The court dismissed the historical laws offered by the State as irrelevant. It categorized them as surety statutes (which required posting a bond only after a complaint), laws that required criminal intent to be a crime, and other unrelated regulations like tax laws. None were analogous to a blanket prohibition on peaceful open carry.
• The Crucial Distinction: Open vs. Concealed Carry: This was the central point of the court's analysis. It rejected the State's argument that historical bans on concealed carry could serve as analogues for a modern ban on open carry. The court explained that the two were not historically interchangeable but were viewed as fundamentally different. Concealed carry was disfavored because it was associated with "sneak attacks" and "secret advantages and unmanly assassinations" (State v. Chandler), or taking an "undue advantage over an unsuspecting adversary" (Sutton v. State). This historical view distinguished it from open carry, which was seen as the proper method for a "manly defence of themselves" (Chandler).
• Antebellum Case Law: To support its reasoning, the court relied heavily on 19th-century state supreme court cases. These historical rulings demonstrated that open carry was consistently viewed as the core, constitutionally protected method of bearing arms. For instance, the Georgia Supreme Court in Nunn v. State (1846) held that "a prohibition against bearing arms openly, is in conflict with the Constitution, and void." Similarly, the Alabama Supreme Court in State v. Reid (1840) concluded that the legislature "cannot inhibit the citizen from bearing arms openly." These cases established a clear historical understanding that while legislatures could regulate concealed carry, a ban on open carry amounted to a denial of the right altogether.
The State's failure to provide a single relevant historical analogue that imposed a burden comparable to Florida's total ban on open carry was fatal to its case under the Bruen standard.
5.0 Holding and Rationale: The Invalidation of Section 790.053
Having found Florida's Open Carry Ban inconsistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation, the court was compelled to find the law unconstitutional. This conclusion was a direct application of the binding precedent set by the U.S. Supreme Court.
The First District Court of Appeal declared section 790.053, Florida Statutes, unconstitutional, reversed Stanley McDaniels's conviction, and vacated his sentence.
The core rationale for this holding was straightforward. Because the Second Amendment's text protects the public carrying of firearms, the State bore the burden of proving that a complete ban on open carry was consistent with historical tradition. The State failed to meet this burden. The historical record reviewed by the court demonstrated that open carry was the default, protected mode of carrying arms for self-defense, while historical restrictions overwhelmingly targeted concealed carry as the practice of those seeking an unfair advantage. The court concluded that what the State may not do is "extinguish the right altogether for ordinary, law-abiding, adult citizens."
This legal holding has immediate, real-world consequences for the state of Florida.
6.0 Immediate Impact and Lingering Legal Boundaries
The legal effect of the McDaniels ruling was immediate and binding on all of Florida's trial courts. A guidance memorandum issued by the Florida Attorney General just days after the decision confirmed its statewide impact, stating, "Effectively, the McDaniels decision is now the law of the State."
Guidance to Law Enforcement and Prosecutors
The Attorney General's memorandum directly advised state authorities on the practical effects of the ruling. Because no Florida court could lawfully convict a defendant for violating the open carry ban, the memorandum stated that "prudence counsels that prosecutors and law enforcement personnel should likewise refrain from arresting or prosecuting law-abiding citizens carrying a firearm in a manner that is visible to others."
What the McDaniels Decision Does Not Change
The ruling, while transformative, operates within a larger framework of existing firearm laws. The Attorney General's guidance clarified several key legal boundaries that remain fully intact:
• Threatening Display: The ruling does not affect Florida Statute § 790.10, which prohibits exhibiting a firearm in a "rude, careless, angry, or threatening manner."
• Prohibited Persons: The state's authority to prohibit felons and other disqualified individuals from possessing firearms is undisturbed.
• Sensitive Locations: The decision does not implicate existing laws, such as § 790.06(12)(a), that restrict carrying firearms—whether openly or concealed—in specific "sensitive locations" like schools, government buildings, or courthouses.
• Private Property Rights: Private property owners retain their legal right to prohibit firearms on their premises. A person carrying a firearm who is asked to leave and refuses to do so can be charged with armed trespass, a third-degree felony.
While McDaniels v. State fundamentally restores the right to open carry in Florida, it does so within a well-established framework of existing laws that continue to regulate the time, place, and manner of firearm possession to ensure public safety.
