**SECTION 1: Understanding the Difference - Seal vs. Expunge (500-600 words)**

*1.1 What is Sealing?*
- Definition: Record still exists but hidden from public view
- Who can still see it: Law enforcement, certain government agencies
- Analogy: "Like putting your file in a locked cabinet vs. shredding it"
- When it's used: Typically for cases that resulted in withhold of adjudication

*1.2 What is Expungement?*
- Definition: Record physically destroyed/removed
- Who can still see it: Very limited (mainly criminal justice agencies with court order)
- Higher standard: Requires case dismissal, acquittal, or no information filed
- Gold standard of record clearing

**SECTION 1: Understanding Domestic Violence Charges in Florida (500-600 words)**

*1.1 What is Domestic Violence? (Legal Definition)*
- Florida Statute 741.28 definition
- Key element: Relationship requirement
  - Spouses or former spouses
  - Persons related by blood or marriage
  - Persons who are presently residing together or have in the past
  - Persons who have a child in common (regardless of marriage)
  - NOT: Dating relationship without cohabitation (different law)

*1.2 Criminal Acts that Qualify as Domestic Violence*
- NOT a separate crime - it's an enhancement
- Underlying offenses:
  - Battery (most common)
  - Assault
  - Aggravated battery
  - Sexual battery
  - Stalking
  - Kidnapping
  - False imprisonment
- Explanation: It's "battery + domestic relationship = domestic battery"

*1.3 Domestic Battery (Misdemeanor) - F.S. 784.03*
- Most common charge
- Definition: Intentionally touching or striking against will, OR causing bodily harm
- Classification: First-degree misdemeanor
- Penalties: Up to 1 year jail, $1,000 fine
- Mandatory minimum: 5 days jail if bodily harm OR prior DV conviction
- NO ability for judge to withhold adjudication (automatic conviction if found guilty)

*1.4 Aggravated Domestic Battery (Felony) - F.S. 784.045*
- When it applies:
  - Intentionally caused great bodily harm, permanent disability, or permanent disfigurement
  - Used a deadly weapon
  - Victim was pregnant and defendant knew or should have known
- Classification: Second-degree felony
- Penalties: Up to 15 years prison, $10,000 fine

*1.5 Other Related Charges*
- Violation of injunction (no contact order)
- Stalking in domestic context
- Criminal mischief (property damage)
- Often charged together

**SECTION 2: The Arrest Process & Immediate Consequences (600-700 words)**

*2.1 Mandatory Arrest Policy*
- Florida's "mandatory arrest" law explained
- Probable cause standard (very low threshold)
- Officers MUST arrest if they believe violence occurred
- Primary aggressor determination (who gets arrested)
- Reality: Often arrests both parties or whoever called 911 second

*2.2 What Happens During Arrest*
- Miranda rights
- Booking process
- Domestic violence intake questions
- Bond considerations
- Insider perspective: What deputies are trained to look for

*2.3 No Contact Order (Automatic)*
- Issued immediately upon arrest
- Cannot contact alleged victim (even if they want contact)
- Cannot return to shared residence (often)
- Violation = new criminal charge
- Duration: Until modified by judge or case resolved
- How to seek modification

*2.4 Bond & Release Conditions*
- First appearance hearing (within 24 hours)
- Bond amount considerations
- Conditions:
  - No contact with victim
  - Stay away from residence
  - Surrender firearms
  - GPS monitoring (in some cases)
  - Batterer's intervention program (BIP)

*2.5 Immediate Life Impacts*
- Cannot go home

- Loss of firearm rights (immediately, even before conviction)

**SECTION 3: Penalties & Long-Term Consequences (500-600 words)**

*3.1 Criminal Penalties*
- Misdemeanor DV: Up to 1 year jail
- Felony DV: Up to 15 years prison
- Fines
- Probation (often with strict conditions)
- Mandatory batterer's intervention program (26-week course, ~$600)

- No early termination of probation allowed

*3.2 Collateral Consequences*
- Permanent criminal record (cannot be sealed or expunged if convicted)
- Loss of firearm rights (federal law - lifetime ban)
- Employment impacts (background checks)
- Professional licenses affected
- Housing/rental applications
- Immigration consequences (deportation risk for non-citizens)
- Security clearances

*3.3 Family Court Implications*
- Custody and visitation impacts
- Domestic violence injunctions (civil restraining orders)
- Presumption against custody if DV finding
- Supervised visitation requirements
- Potential loss of parental rights in extreme cases

**SECTION 4: Common Defense Strategies (700-800 words)**

*4.1 Self-Defense*
- Florida's self-defense law applies to DV cases
- Elements: Reasonable belief of imminent harm
- Proportional response
- Burden: State must disprove self-defense beyond reasonable doubt
- Stand Your Ground applicability

*4.2 Defense of Others*
- Protecting children or other family members
- Same standards as self-defense

*4.3 Mutual Combat*
- Both parties fighting
- May result in both being charged
- Doesn't eliminate your case but affects credibility/severity

*4.4 False Accusations*
- More common than people think
- Motivations:
  - Custody/divorce advantage
  - Anger/revenge
  - Mental health issues
  - Manipulation to control situation
- Evidence to prove:
  - Inconsistent statements
  - No visible injuries
  - Lack of 911 call immediacy
  - History of false allegations
  - Witness testimony

*4.5 Lack of Evidence*
- State's burden: Prove beyond reasonable doubt
- Common evidence problems:
  - No witnesses
  - No injuries
  - No medical treatment
  - Conflicting statements
  - Recanting victim (complicated, but relevant)

*4.6 Accidental Contact*
- Not "intentional" touching
- Example: Heated argument, hand gestures, accidental contact
- Lack of intent element

*4.7 Exaggeration of Minor Incident*
- Blown out of proportion
- What really happened vs. what's alleged
- Body camera footage often helps here

*4.8 Challenging the "Domestic Relationship"*
- If no qualifying relationship, not DV (different charge)
- Reduces penalties significantly
- Example: Dating but never lived together = not domestic

**SECTION 5: The Role of the "Victim" (400-500 words)**

*5.1 Can Charges Be Dropped if Victim Recants?*
- Short answer: Not automatically

**SECTION 1: Florida's Leaving the Scene Laws (600-700 words)**

*1.1 Legal Requirements After an Accident*
- Florida Statute 316.062 (crash involving injury/death)
- Florida Statute 316.063 (crash involving property damage)
- Legal duties:
  - Stop immediately at scene OR as close as possible
  - Remain at scene
  - Render aid if anyone injured
  - Exchange information (name, address, vehicle registration, driver's license)
  - Show license to other driver/officer/injured person
  - Report crash to law enforcement if required

*1.2 When You Must Report*
- Injury to any person
- Death
- Property damage appears to exceed $500

*1.3 Leaving the Scene with Injury - F.S. 316.027*
- THIRD-DEGREE FELONY
- Penalties: Up to 5 years prison, $5,000 fine
- Minimum mandatory: None (unless serious bodily injury)
- Driver's license suspension: 3 years minimum

*1.4 Leaving the Scene with Serious Bodily Injury*
- SECOND-DEGREE FELONY
- Penalties: Up to 15 years prison
- Mandatory minimum: 2 years prison (if convicted)
- Driver's license revocation: Minimum 3 years

*1.5 Leaving the Scene with Death*
- FIRST-DEGREE FELONY
- Penalties: Up to 30 years prison
- Mandatory minimum: 4 years prison
- Potential for life sentence if death was caused by defendant
- Driver's license revocation: Permanent

*1.6 Leaving the Scene - Property Damage Only*
- SECOND-DEGREE MISDEMEANOR (if under $50 damage)
- FIRST-DEGREE MISDEMEANOR (if over $50 damage - almost always)
- Penalties: Up to 1 year jail, $1,000 fine
- Driver's license suspension: 6 months to 3 years
- Most common charge

**SECTION 2: Why People Leave the Scene (300-400 words)**

*2.1 Fear of DUI Investigation*
- Most common reason (from my experience as FHP)
- Belief that leaving avoids DUI charge
- Reality: Hit and run + DUI charges often filed together
- Worse penalties for both

*2.2 Unaware of Accident*
- Legitimately didn't know contact occurred
- Common with:
  - Large vehicles (trucks, SUVs)
  - Minor contact in parking lots
  - Road debris vs. vehicle contact
  - Highway speed incidents
- Valid defense if true

*2.3 Panic and Confusion*
- Shock from crash
- Injured and not thinking clearly
- First-time accident, didn't know what to do
- Fear of other driver (road rage situation)

*2.4 No Insurance/Suspended License*
- Fear of additional charges
- Thinking it's "better" to leave
- Reality: Makes everything worse

*2.5 Underage or Parents' Car*

**SECTION 4: Common Defenses (700-800 words)**

*4.1 Lack of Knowledge*
- Didn't know contact occurred
- Burden: State must prove you knew or should have known
- Factors:
  - How minor was contact?
  - Road/traffic conditions
  - Vehicle size
  - Where on vehicle was contact?
- Example: Shopping cart vs. your truck in parking lot
- Body camera analysis can help prove this

*4.2 Failure to Prove You Were the Driver*
- State must prove YOU were driving
- Not enough to prove you own the vehicle
- Multiple people have access to car
- Refuse to make statement identifying yourself as driver
- Car was stolen/loaned/etc.

*4.3 Actual Emergency*
- Had to leave scene due to legitimate emergency
- Examples:
  - Medical emergency (yours or passenger's)
  - Feeling unsafe (threat from other driver)
  - Natural disaster
- BUT: Must report accident ASAP (called 911 or went to hospital/police)

*4.4 Not Actually a "Crash"*
- Contact didn't rise to level requiring stop
- Examples:
  - Normal traffic bump (under 5 mph, no damage)
  - Incidental contact with zero damage
- Rarely successful but sometimes applicable

*4.5 You Did Stop and Exchange Info*
- Misunderstanding or miscommunication
- You stopped, other party waved you on
- Exchanged information but other party lost it/gave false info
- Prove with:
  - Photos you took of info exchanged
  - Witness testimony
  - Your contemporaneous actions (tried to report, called insurance, etc.)

*4.6 Insufficient Evidence*
- State can't prove case beyond reasonable doubt
- Weak vehicle identification
- No witness to actual impact
- Could have been many similar vehicles
- Timeline doesn't match

*4.7 Involuntary Intoxication / Diminished Capacity*
- Rarely used but possible
- Head injury from crash causing confusion
- Diabetic episode
- Other medical emergency affecting judgment

**SECTION 5: What to Do If You Left the Scene (400-500 words)**

*5.1 Immediately After Realizing Your Mistake*
- DO: Call an attorney before doing anything else
- DO: Document the damage to your vehicle (photos)
- DO: Write down what you remember while fresh
- DON'T: Go back to scene (may be unsafe)
- DON'T: Contact the other party directly
- DON'T: Call police without attorney advice

*5.2 Should You Turn Yourself In?*
- Pros:
  - Shows good faith
  - Negotiating leverage
  - May avoid arrest warrant
  - Can potentially reduce charges
- Cons:
  - May incriminate yourself
  - May not be charged yet
  - Lose ability to challenge driver ID
- ALWAYS consult attorney first

*5.3 Making a Report Through Your Attorney*
- Attorney can facilitate
- Potentially avoid arrest
- Control the narrative
- Provide explanation with legal protection

*5.4 If Police Contact You*
- DO: Be polite and respectful
- DO: Say "I want to speak with my attorney before answering questions"
- DON'T: Admit you were driving
- DON'T: Explain what happened
- DON'T: Let them search your vehicle without warrant
- Insider tip: They may minimize severity to get you talking - don't fall for it

**SECTION 6: License Suspension & Hardship License (300-400 words)**

*6.1 Administrative Suspension*
- DHSMV will suspend license upon conviction
- Minimum: 6 months (property damage)
- Up to: Permanent (death cases)
- Suspension is separate from criminal penalties

*6.2 Hardship License Eligibility*
- May be eligible after completing suspension period
- Business purposes only (BPO) or employment purposes only (EPO)
- Requirements:
  - Complete DUI school (yes, even for non-DUI hit and run)
  - Pay reinstatement fees
  - SR-22 insurance
  - Hearing with DHSMV
- Attorney can help with application

*6.3 Impact on CDL Holders*
- Permanent disqualification possible
- Career-ending consequences
- Fighting the charge is critical

**SECTION 7: Insurance Implications (200-300 words)**

- Carrier may deny coverage for hit and run
- Rates will increase significantly if convicted
- Civil liability remains (victim can sue)
- Uninsured motorist coverage on victim's policy may apply
- SR-22 requirement after license reinstatement

**SECTION 8: Plea Negotiations & Alternatives (300-400 words)**

*8.1 Reducing to Lesser Charge*
- Property damage instead of injury (if evidence weak)
- Misdemeanor instead of felony
- Careless driving or other traffic offense

*8.2 Pre-Trial Diversion*
- May be available for first-time offenders (property damage only)
- Complete conditions, case dismissed
- Typically requires admission and restitution

*8.3 Trial*
- When state's case is weak
- Knowledge or driver ID issues
- High stakes (CDL, professional license, immigration)

- Knowledge and driver ID are key elements to challenge
- Even if you left scene, legal options exist
- Critical to act fast (evidence collection, attorney consultation)
- Action steps
