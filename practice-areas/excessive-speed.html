What Qualifies as "Dangerous Excessive Speed"?

While any speeding is negligent, dangerous excessive speed goes beyond a simple traffic infraction. It is often classified as criminal recklessness. In many jurisdictions, this includes:

  • Velocities exceeding 100 mph
  • Driving 50+ mph over the posted limit
  • Racing or "drag racing" on public roads
  • Aggressive weaving at high speeds

At these velocities, safety features fail. Airbags and crumple zones are not designed to withstand impacts at 100+ mph. The result is rarely a "fender bender"—it is often a life-altering tragedy.

The Physics of a High-Speed Crash

Why are these accidents so much worse than standard collisions? It comes down to physics.

1. Reaction Time Evaporates

A vehicle traveling at 100 mph covers a football field in roughly two seconds. The driver has literally zero time to react to a braking car or a pedestrian.

2. Impact Force Multiplies

The energy released in a crash increases exponentially with speed. A crash at 75 mph involves double the energy of a crash at 50 mph.

3. Safety Failure

Seatbelts can snap, and engines can be pushed into the passenger cabin.

Legal Note

Proving excessive speed often requires more than just witness testimony. We utilize black box data (EDR), skid mark analysis, and forensic accident reconstruction to prove exactly how fast the defendant was traveling at the moment of impact.

Establishing Liability and Seeking Damages

In cases of dangerous excessive speed, the concept of "Negligence Per Se" often applies. This means that because the driver was violating a safety law (speed limits) designed to protect the public, they are automatically presumed negligent.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if the insurance company says I was partially at fault?

We aggressively challenge comparative fault arguments. Even if you bear some responsibility, Florida's comparative negligence laws allow you to recover damages proportional to the other party's fault.

How do you prove the other driver was speeding excessively?

We use multiple evidence sources including event data recorders (black boxes), accident reconstruction, skid mark analysis, witness testimony, surveillance footage, and cell phone records to establish speed at impact.

How long do I have to file a claim?

In Florida, you generally have four years to file a personal injury lawsuit from the date of the accident. However, it's critical to contact an attorney immediately to preserve evidence.

Do you handle wrongful death cases?

Yes. If you've lost a loved one due to an excessive speed crash, we can help your family pursue a wrongful death claim to recover damages for your loss.

What does "contingency fee basis" mean?

It means you don't pay any attorney fees unless we win your case. Our fee comes from the settlement or verdict we obtain for you.
